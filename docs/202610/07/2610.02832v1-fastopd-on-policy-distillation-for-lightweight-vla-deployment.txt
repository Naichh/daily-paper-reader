## ABSTRACT 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) foundation models have scaled rapidly to en-hance manipulation performance and generalizability, but this scaling incurs high computational costs that render real-world deployment increasingly challenging. Existing approaches typically mitigate this issue by designing smaller architec-tures or reducing the iterative denoising steps in flow-based policies. In this work, we propose FastOPD , a foundation-to-lightweight VLA framework that enables the practical deployment of large-scale VLAs through efficient on-policy distilla-tion. Specifically, FastOPD adapts a flow map for single-state teacher supervision and combines it with a self-consistency objective to construct a compact student that learns the teacher dynamics. Furthermore, we theoretically demonstrate that minimizing this objective allows the distilled student to recover a distribution on par with that induced by an ideal few-step teacher model. We evaluate FastOPD across diverse foundation policies in simulation and real-world experiments. On LIBERO, FastOPD retains 84% of the performance of π0.5 with only two infer-ence steps, reducing inference latency by 78.1% while outperforming existing few-step distillation baselines in average success rate. With LingBot-VLA as the teacher, FastOPD improves the single-step success rate over the base student by 15.9 percentage points on RoboTwin 2.0. We further demonstrate its applicabil-ity to a World Action Model (WAM) and deploy a compact student distilled from MolmoAct2 on a real robot. Vision-Language Model

Recent progress in robot learning has moved toward large-scale foundation policies, ranging from Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Collaboration, 2024; Black et al., 2025b;a; Bjorck et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026a) to emerging World Action Models (WAMs) (Kim et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). However, these powerful foundation priors also lead to increasingly large policy backbones and high computational cost, creating a tension between model capability and deployment efficiency on physical robots. Lightweight VLA models such as SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025) address the efficiency side of this trade-off by directly designing smaller policies with less than 0.5B parameters. A complementary direction is to transfer the capability of a powerful foundation policy into a com-pact student through knowledge distillation. Knowledge Distillation (KD) (Hinton et al., 2015; Mansourian et al., 2025) has been widely studied for transferring the capability of a large teacher model into a smaller student. Specifically, On-Policy Distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024) su-pervises the student using teacher predictions on its own generated trajectories, reducing distribution mismatch and compounding errors from offline distillation. OPD also provides dense supervision along the student trajectory, in contrast to the sparse rewards commonly used in reinforcement learn-ing. While OPD has been actively explored in LLM post-training, recent works have extended it to diffusion and flow models (Li et al., 2026a; Fang et al., 2026b). However, directly applying OPD to flow-based policies is computationally inefficient. It requires rolling out the student’s denoising trajectory and repeatedly evaluating the teacher at the visited timesteps, resulting in high training cost for large foundation teachers. In this work, we revisit few-step flow-map models through the lens of on-policy distillation (OPD) and introduce FastOPD , a foundation-to-lightweight VLA framework for efficient policy distilla-tion. Our goal is to transfer the capabilities of a large-scale VLA into a compact student while substantially reducing both training and inference costs, without sacrificing policy performance (see Fig. 1). To reduce the training cost of conventional OPD, FastOPD combines on-policy teacher su-pervision of the student’s velocity field with a self-consistency objective (Frans et al., 2025). Rather than querying teacher supervision throughout an entire generation trajectory, the self-consistency objective propagates local velocity supervision to finite-interval shortcut transitions, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This allows the student to approximate the teacher dynamics without explicitly constructing or evaluating a teacher flow map over each shortcut transition. We further show theoretically that FastOPD implicitly recovers the distribution induced by the teacher’s ideal shortcut model, provid-ing a distributional justification for few-step distillation. Importantly, the same self-consistency formulation naturally induces an any-step flow map (Boffi et al., 2024; Song et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Geng et al., 2025; Sabour et al., 2025), allow-ing the distilled student to operate with substantially fewer sampling steps at inference. FastOPD therefore compresses the foundation VLA along two complementary dimensions: model capacity and sampling complexity, enabling deployment on resource-constrained, consumer-grade hardware. The proposed formulation also enables stable training in the challenging few-step regime without adversarial objectives or costly higher-order derivative computations. A concurrent work, VLA-OPD (Zhong et al., 2026), also studies on-policy distillation for VLA models, but primarily uses expert supervision to improve policy performance. In contrast, FastOPD focuses on transferring a foundation policy into a lightweight few-step student. We evaluate FastOPD on two simulation benchmarks and one real-world manipulation task, using diverse foundation teachers with 3B–6B parameters (Black et al., 2025a; Yuan et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026a). To maximize both architectural and inference efficiency, we adopt SmolVLA as 2Preprint. the lightweight student policy. We compare against representative few-step distillation methods, including CTM, DMD, and iMF. Across these settings, FastOPD enables the compact student to recover up to 84% of the performance of teacher models that are more than 7.3× larger, using only one or two sampling steps. This substantially reduces inference cost while retaining a large fraction of the foundation policy’s performance. To the best of our knowledge, FastOPD is the first OPD framework for VLAs that jointly reduces both model size and the number of sampling steps while retaining the capabilities of a large-scale foundation policy.

Vision-Language-Action Models. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Collaboration, 2024; Black et al., 2025b;a; Bjorck et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024; Octo Model Team et al., 2024) couple a pretrained vision-language backbone with an action decoding head to map an obser-vation and a language instruction to a distribution over robot actions, most commonly via a flow or diffusion action expert (Chi et al., 2023; Black et al., 2025b) trained with flow matching loss. Recent foundation VLA models illustrate the scale required for strong multi-task generalization, typically exceeding 2B parameters and trained on over 10,000 hours of multimodal robot data spanning mul-tiple embodiments (Black et al., 2025b;a; Wu et al., 2026), but remain expensive to deploy. Several works (Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Shukor et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025; Reuss et al., 2025) instead train smaller VLAs; SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025), for example, reduces model size to 450 million parameters by using a small VLM and layer skipping. However, it still requires N =10 

denoising steps per action chunk and does not benefit from the capabilities of larger foundation policies. 

Flow Map Models. A wide range of few-step generative methods aim to reduce iterative sampling cost. Distillation-based approaches (Sabour et al., 2025; Boffi et al., 2024), including Consistency Models (CM) (Song et al., 2023), and Consistency Trajectory Models (CTM) (Kim et al., 2024), distill a pre-trained multi-step teacher into a few-step student, while score-distillation methods di-rectly match teacher and student distributions (Yin et al., 2024b;a). Distillation-free approaches, such as MeanFlow (MF) (Geng et al., 2025) and Shortcut Models (Frans et al., 2025), instead train from scratch using self-consistency constraints. On-policy Distillation. Given a pretrained teacher diffusion model, on-policy distillation (OPD) supervises the student to match the teacher’s one-step transition kernel pT (· | xti ) along the student’s own rollout x0: N ∼ pS,θ , by minimizing the reverse KL objective (Li et al., 2026a). Since the student and teacher share the same diffusion coefficient, resulting in identical covari-ance, its per-step KL reduces to a closed-form squared distance between the means, KL( pS ∥pT ) ∝∥μS (xti ; θ) − μT (xti )∥22. Despite providing dense supervision, this objective necessitates evalu-ating the teacher at every single denoising step, resulting in computational inefficiency. FastOPD circumvents this training bottleneck by supervising the student at a single on-policy state per trajec-tory. Formulated within a few-step flow map regime, the distilled policy is naturally equipped for real-time robot control. 

## 3 FAST OPD: F AST ON-P OLICY DISTILLATION 

Overview. Our goal is to efficiently distill a large pretrained teacher vϕ into a compact flow map stu-dent uθ , while achieving performance on par with the teacher. Specifically, we parameterize the flow map as fθ (x, s, t ) = x + ( t − s)uθ (x, s, t ), which satisfies the boundary condition fθ (x, s, s ) = x.We denote its diagonal by vθ (x, t ) := uθ (x, t, t ), which corresponds to the instantaneous velocity. As illustrated in Fig. 3, we embed s and t independently via sinusoidal positional encoding and fuse them through an additional projection layer ψ(·). Here, we omit the condition c for brevity. The key idea of FastOPD is to match the teacher only at sampled on-policy states and propagate this local velocity supervision to finite-step flow maps through self-consistency. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Our experiments span two multitask simulation benchmarks (Sec. 4.1) and one real-robot task (Sec. 4.2). We freeze the vision–language backbone and fine-tune only the action expert and projec-tions with the additional time projection layer described in Sec. 3. We evaluate each method using 1, 2, 4, and 10 denoising steps and compare it against the teacher and the initial student with 50 trials per task. Full experimental details are provided in Appendix B. 4.1 SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS 

Benchmarks. We evaluate FastOPD on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025). LIBERO evaluates single-arm manipulation across four standard evaluation suites—Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long—comprising 40 tasks in total. RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025) comprises 50 challenging bimanual manipulation tasks, with 2,500 demonstrations collected in clean scenes and 25,000 demonstrations collected under heavy scene randomization. We report results in both clean and randomized settings on unseen environments. 

Baselines. For simulation experiments, we compare our method with three representative ap-proaches to few-step generation: Consistency Trajectory Model (CTM) (Kim et al., 2024), Dis-tribution Matching Distillation (DMD) (Yin et al., 2024b;a), and improved Mean Flow (iMF) (Geng et al., 2026). Since iMF was originally formulated for training from scratch, we adapt it to the distil-lation setting by replacing its self-referential instantaneous velocity target with the teacher velocity. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we present FastOPD, an efficient on-policy distillation framework that transfers large-scale VLAs into lightweight student models to maximize computational efficiency without com-promising performance. Specifically, FastOPD streamlines the iterative denoising process by an-choring a single on-policy state to the teacher, coupled with a self-consistency objective for finite-interval transitions. In simulation and real-robot experiments across diverse VLA and WAM teach-ers, FastOPD improves over the base student in the few-step regime and outperforms existing few-step distillation methods, while substantially reducing training and inference cost. We hope this framework facilitates scalable, real-time deployment of foundation policies on physical robots. 9Preprint.

## C ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTS 

Connection with previous works. Although SnapFlow (Luan et al., 2026) adopts a two-step consis-tency target similar to ours, the two objectives differ in both their anchor and their scope. SnapFlow is a self-distillation method that combines flow matching on ground-truth actions with a shortcut loss defined only on the fixed interval from t = 1 to t = 0 :

LSnapFlow = λ1 LFM + (1 − λ1)λ2 Lshortcut , Lshortcut = uθ (x1, 1, 0 | c) − vtgt 

> 22

, (41) where vtgt = 12

sg[ vθ (x1, 1 | c)] + sg[ vθ ( ˆ x0.5, 0.5 | c)]  and ˆx0.5 = x1 − 0.5 sg[ vθ (x1, 1 | c)] . In contrast, FastOPD enforces self-consistency over arbitrary intervals (s, t ), yielding a flow map that supports any number of sampling steps, and anchors the target to the teacher through OPFD rather than to the data through flow matching. SnapFlow can thus be viewed as a teacher-free counterpart of our objective restricted to a single interval, which allows us to isolate the contribution of teacher supervision. 

Student initialization. In Sec. 4.3, we studied on-policy distillation (OPD) as an alternative initial-ization strategy for the flow map. Specifically, we first train the SmolVLA base model with OPD for 5,000 iterations (Stage 1), and then fine-tune it into a flow map with the FastOPD objective for 10,000 iterations (Stage 2). Tab. 12 reports the per-suite results, and Fig. 10 shows the success rate over training iterations. As in Fig. 4, FastOPD consistently improves performance over the course of training under this OPD-based initialization as well.
