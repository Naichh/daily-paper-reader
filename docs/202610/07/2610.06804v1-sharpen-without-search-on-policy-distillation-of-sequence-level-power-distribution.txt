## ABSTRACT 

A language model can give a correct answer more probability than any single incorrect answer and still usually sample an incorrect one, because the incorrect answers together hold more probability. The power distribution raises each com-plete answer’s probability to a power above one and renormalizes, shifting prob-ability toward answers the model finds most likely (sharpening). Sampling from it improves reasoning without changing parameters, but needs many scored can-didates per query. We show that a model can instead be trained to produce such answers in one generation. On-policy power distillation ( OPPD ) runs a sequential Monte Carlo sampler in which the model being trained generates candidates and a frozen teacher’s power distribution weights them; the same probabilities weight each answer in a maximum-likelihood update. Training raises single-generation accuracy by up to 23 .0 points on MATH500 and 27 .3 on GSM8K over the un-trained model at the same temperature, and one generation scores 2.4 and 3.5

points above published power sampling with 64 candidates, recovering 94% of the gain that 16 candidates give the untrained model. For context, against GRPO trained with verified rewards from the same checkpoint and budget, OPPD scores 

3.8, 4.0 and 5.4 points higher on MATH500, GSM8K and AIME using no refer-ence answers; the two are complementary, and OPPD applied after GRPO adds up to 9.3 points. Trained only on mathematics, OPPD raises HumanEval accuracy by up to 5.3 points. One loss coefficient moves the sharpening exponent the model absorbs between 1.19 and 2.02 , against 1.14 for ordinary on-policy distillation, and it rises mostly on the model’s own answers. Gains hold across model families and sizes, including a model already trained with verified rewards, where lower-ing the temperature gives nothing and OPPD adds 4.4 points on MATH500. Code: 

https://github.com/ArminAzizi98/OPPD .

66 .8% when it generates one answer and 79 .2% when a sampler with sixteen candidates draws approximate samples from πα. This sampler, however, has to run again for every query. In this paper we ask whether this cost can be paid once, during training. Ordinary distillation trains a student to match a teacher’s next-token probabilities. The next-token probabilities of πα, however, depend on all possible continuations of the prefix, so no teacher forward pass computes them. Sam-ples from πα can therefore only be drawn approximately, by a sampler run at inference time. Our method, on-policy power distillation ( OPPD ), trains the student on the output of such a sampler, the sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) sampler of Azizi et al. (2026). This sampler extends N candidate answers in blocks of tokens and gives each candidate an importance weight, which measures how much more probable it is under πα than under the distribution that generated it. When these weights become too uneven, it replaces low-weight candidates with copies of high-weight ones. In OPPD the student generates the candidates and a frozen teacher defines πα; in self-distillation, the teacher is a copy of the initial student. The teacher probabilities that set the importance weights also give each complete answer a training weight, and the student is updated by weighted maximum likelihood on its own answers (Figure 1). A second loss term, the token-level anchor with coefficient λ, keeps the student’s next-token distributions near those of the teacher at temperature 1/α .

Contributions. (1) A training method that uses no reference answers, verifier or reward model, in which one teacher forward pass per block of tokens supplies both the importance weights and the training weights. (2) A trained model that replaces the sampler, scoring above published power sampling with 64 candidates on the same model and obtaining 94% of the gain that sixteen candi-dates give the untrained model. ## 2 BACKGROUND 

2.1 SHARPENING COMPLETE ANSWERS VERSUS SHARPENING TOKENS 

Lowering the sampling temperature is cheaper and may appear to have the same effect, but the two operations differ. Sampling at temperature τ is token-level : it replaces each next-token distribution by one proportional to its probabilities raised to the power 1/τ , renormalizing at every position. The power distribution is sequence-level : (1) renormalizes once, over complete answers. Temperature therefore compares next tokens without information about the answers they lead to, while the power distribution gives little total probability to answers that begin with a prefix whose continuations are all uncertain. In the two-token example of Appendix A, the power distribution at α = 2 places 0.213

The student generates N = 16 candidates at temperature 1/α in blocks of ∆ = 64 tokens. The teacher scores all N ∆ new tokens of a block in one forward pass, which reduces the sequential teacher calls for a T -token answer from T to T / ∆. Three details affect correctness: the importance and training weights use separate accumulators; recorded training weights are renormalized after ev-ery resampling step, so that duplicated candidates are not counted more than once (Appendix Q); and finished candidates are charged the mean per-token decrease in log importance weight of unfinished ones, so that stopping early does not raise a candidate’s weight (Appendix P). 5Preprint 

## 4 MEASURING ABSORBED SHARPENING 

Higher accuracy alone does not show that the student moved toward πα, so we test a consequence that is specific to this movement. Let pbase be the model the student was initialized from, and let a context c be a prompt with a prefix. If training sharpened the student relative to pbase with some exponent a, so that pϕ ≈ p a

> base

/Z , then 

log pϕ(v | c) = a log pbase (v | c) − log Z(c), (10) which is a line of slope a in log pbase (v | c) at each context c. A slope of 1 means that no sharp-ening was absorbed, and a slope of α means that the student equals pbase at temperature 1/α .We estimate the slope by least squares of log pϕ on log pbase over the 200 tokens most likely under pbase , weighted by pbase (v | c) so that the near-zero tail does not dominate. We report the median slope across contexts as the absorbed exponent αeff (pbase → pϕ). In self-distillation 

αeff (pbase → pT) = 1 .000 exactly, so no measured increase can come from copying a sharper model; with a larger teacher, the control is ordinary on-policy distillation against the same teacher. The slope is dominated by noise when pbase is close to deterministic, so we report αeff only where the entropy of pbase is large enough for the fit to separate the models, and a direct entropy measure-ment elsewhere. Appendix M gives both and validates the estimator on synthetic logits.

5.2 COMPARISON WITH TRAINING ON VERIFIED REWARDS 

GRPO shows how large a gain is usually available when reference answers can be used. We train the two methods from the same checkpoint to put OPPD on that scale, not to argue that one should replace the other, and they compose. We train OPPD and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) from the same Qwen3-8B-Base checkpoint for 

300 optimizer steps each. At each step, both draw two prompts and sample sixteen answers of at most 1024 tokens per prompt, so each generates 9,600 answers. Our GRPO is the standard version, with one optimization pass over each batch of samples and a binary reward from the evaluation grader. Both runs are small next to published GRPO training, 600 prompt draws of at most 1024 

tokens each, so the comparison bounds neither method at a larger budget. OPPD scores higher on all three benchmarks (Table 13), by 3.75 points on MATH500, 4.00 on GSM8K and 5.42 on AIME, each between one and two standard errors and all three with the same sign. (Appendix B.10). The two methods also combine. Applied to a Qwen2.5-Math-7B checkpoint that GRPO had already trained from 50 .25 to 64 .00 on MATH500 and 59 .25 to 71 .00 on GSM8K, OPPD adds 9.25

## 6 RELATED WORK 

Power sampling and sequential Monte Carlo. Karan & Du (2026) introduce Metropolis– Hastings sampling from the power distribution (1) and show that it matches reinforcement-learning post-training on reasoning benchmarks. Azizi et al. (2026) replace the serial Markov chain with the batch-parallel sampler we use, and Ji et al. (2026) approximate the next-token distribution of πα

with sampled continuations. All three methods run at inference time. Tomihari & Sato (2026) show that the power distribution is the closed-form optimum of KL-regularized reinforcement learning when the reward is the model’s own sequence log-probability, and derive power self-distillation, which trains a model on samples a power sampler produced and reaches accuracy at or above power sampling at much lower inference cost. Moving the cost of power sampling into training is there-fore established. OPPD differs in where the training answers come from and in what is optimized: the student is the sampler’s proposal distribution, so the answers are its own and one teacher pass per block serves both the sampler and the update, and the objective is the R´ enyi family (3) rather than supervised learning on stored samples. OPPD trains on-policy, the setting for which on-policy distillation was introduced (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). SMC has also been used to steer language models toward constraints (Lew et al., 2023) and for inference with learned twist func-tions (Zhao et al., 2024), and Hu et al. (2024) learn to sample intractable posteriors with GFlowNet objectives. We do not learn a twist function (Section 2.2). 

Distillation and post-training. Sequence-level knowledge distillation trains a student on answers generated by the teacher (Kim & Rush, 2016; Hinton et al., 2015), and on-policy variants train on answers the student generates and the teacher scores. Methods that use verified rewards, such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), need to know whether each answer is correct. Methods that reward a model’s own confidence instead include entropy minimization (Agarwal et al., 2025), self-certainty (Zhao et al., 2025), negative token entropy (Prabhudesai et al., 2025) and majority-vote rewards (Zuo et al., 2025), and Huang et al. (2025) analyze self-improvement as sharpening against the model’s own judgments. With the proposal of Theorem 1 and β = 1 , (5) makes our training weight a softmax over the negative R´ enyi entropy accumulated along each answer, a confidence weighting whose scores come from a frozen teacher and are used inside a sampler. OPPD needs only the questions, so it applies where those methods cannot be run, and it can also be applied to a model those methods have already trained. Yue et al. (2025) argue that such methods mostly re-weight answers the model could already produce, as power sampling does.

## A THE TWO -TOKEN EXAMPLE 

Section 2.1 states that sharpening complete answers differs from sharpening tokens. The following example shows the difference on a small case. Consider a vocabulary of two tokens and answers of length two, with p(a) = 0 .6, p(a | a) = 0 .9

and p(a | b) = p(b | b) = 0 .5. Multiplying gives p(aa ) = 0 .54 , p(ab ) = 0 .06 and p(ba ) = p(bb ) = 0.20 . Setting α = 2 :

The power distribution assigns less probability to answers beginning with b, 0.213 against 0.308 .It does so because every continuation of b is uncertain, so no answer beginning with b can reach a high total probability. Temperature cannot represent this, because at the first token it compares 0.6

against 0.4 with no information about the answers those two tokens lead to. The power distribution accounts for how probable the continuations of each prefix are, and temperature does not. This is the information that OPPD transfers into the model parameters.

What is held equal. Both methods run for 300 optimizer steps. At each step both draw two prompts and sample sixteen answers of at most 1024 tokens per prompt, so each method generates 

9,600 answers over the run. GRPO additionally uses a reference answer for every training question, which OPPD never does. Our GRPO implementation is the standard one, with a single optimization pass over each batch of samples. For each prompt it samples sixteen answers, called a group, and scores each with a binary reward from the grader used for evaluation. The advantage of each answer is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation. With a single pass, the model that generated the samples is the model being updated, so the ratio between the new and the sampling probabilities is one and the clipping range is inactive. 

OPPD scores higher on all three benchmarks (Figure 6), by 3.75 points on MATH500, 4.00 on GSM8K and 5.42 on AIME. Groups that give GRPO no training signal. GRPO learns from a group only when the rewards in it differ. If all sixteen answers earn the same reward, the group mean equals every reward, all ad-vantages are zero, and the prompt contributes no gradient. Our logs record how often this happened: 

234 of GRPO’s 600 prompt draws ( 39% ) had uniform rewards and produced no gradient. These draws still cost computation, because the answers had to be generated before their rewards could be compared. This fraction is higher than the average reward rate would suggest. Late in training, the mean reward ranged from 0.06 to 0.97 across steps. If every answer succeeded independently at the average of these rates, fewer than 2% of groups of sixteen would have uniform rewards. Observing 39% 

therefore means that difficulty is not spread evenly across prompts. A given problem tends to be either easy for the model, so that most of the sixteen answers succeed, or too hard, so that almost none do. Both cases tend to give uniform rewards and zero advantages, so mainly problems of intermediate difficulty provide a learning signal.

B.14 WHERE THE SAMPLER ’S GAIN COMES FROM 

Grading every candidate separates two things that the sampler does: it discards candidates while answers are being written, and at the end it re-ranks the finished answers by importance weight. If the importance weights carried information about correctness, the total normalized weight on correct candidates would exceed the fraction of candidates that are correct. Across eleven measurements spanning three models and three numbers of candidates, the two agree to within 0.5 points in every case. This includes self-distillation, where teacher and student share an architecture. Choosing the answer by weighted draw, by highest weight, or by majority vote gives the same result throughout (Appendix R). The teacher’s probabilities therefore carry no useful information for ranking finished answers. All of the sampler’s gain comes from discarding candidates while the answers are still being written and their continuations still differ. Once the answers are complete, the teacher’s probabilities no longer separate correct from incorrect candidates. This has a consequence for a family of methods: re-ranking complete answers by model probability should not be expected to help, whereas intervening during generation should.

Computing the weights incrementally. When importance sampling is applied to complete an-swers in one step, the weights vary over many orders of magnitude and one sample dominates. Defining unnormalized distributions over prefixes, γt(y1: t) = pT(y1: t | x)α, and updating the weights by (2) gives the same total weight γT /q , while letting the sampler monitor the weights and resample during generation. 

Resampling. When the ESS falls below κN (we use κ = 0 .5), the candidates are resampled with probability proportional to their normalized importance weights, and all weights are reset to be equal. We use systematic resampling, which is unbiased and has lower variance than multinomial resampling (Doucet & Johansen, 2009). Duplicated candidates diverge again because their next 22 Preprint tokens are sampled independently. Any unbiased resampling scheme leaves the distribution being approximated unchanged and affects only the variance and the diversity of the candidates. 

What the weights select for. Equation (5) states that, under the variance-optimal proposal, a can-didate’s log importance weight is (1 − α) times the sum of the R´ enyi entropies of the teacher’s next-token distributions along its answer. Since α > 1 makes (1 − α) negative, candidates whose prefixes the teacher is confident about keep their weight, and candidates that pass through uncertain positions lose it. No reward model, verifier or reference answer is used at any point.

## N WHY THE STUDENT AT TEMPERATURE 1 FAILS AS A PROPOSAL 

Section 3.3 states that using the student at temperature 1 as the proposal distribution makes the sampler fail. The second term of (6), (α − 1) log pϕ(yt), has a standard deviation of about 4.6 nats per token at α = 4 . Across a block of ∆ = 64 tokens, the spread between candidates is therefore about 4.6√64 ≈ 37 nats, against a usable range of log N = log 16 ≈ 2.8 nats. With this proposal, 

ESS /N stays at 1/N from the first block onward, which makes the sampler equivalent to selecting one candidate at random. The failure raises no error and produces no unusual value in the training log. The normalized importance weights are a softmax over the log importance weights, so a gap of 

35 nats between the two largest gives the second a relative weight of e−35 ≈ 6 × 10 −16 . Resampling then replaces all N candidates with copies of one. From that point on, the candidates carry a single distinct answer and no longer approximate πα, and the estimator of Theorem 2 has a single sample. With the student at temperature 1/α as the proposal, this term cancels exactly, and the measured 

ESS /N during training lies between 0.39 and 0.64 for every model we train. As a check on the algebra, setting the student equal to the teacher makes the teacher–student differ-ence term vanish, so by Theorem 1 the incremental importance weights should be identical for every candidate. The measured ESS /N in this configuration is 0.998 .

## P CORRECTING THE WEIGHT ADVANTAGE OF STOPPING EARLY 

Equation (5) implies that the log importance weight decreases at every token. A candidate that emits the end-of-sequence token keeps its weight fixed while unfinished candidates continue to lose weight, so stopping early gains weight for reasons unrelated to answer quality. For long chains of reasoning, this would train the model to stop reasoning early, and the loss curve would give no sign of it. We charge each finished candidate the mean per-token increment in log importance weight of the unfinished ones. This is the same constant for every finished candidate at a given step, so the normalized weights among unfinished candidates are unchanged, and only the comparison between finished and unfinished candidates is corrected. As a diagnostic, we use the mean answer length weighted by the normalized importance weights minus the unweighted mean length, which should be near zero. We measure this diagnostic in a controlled setting where student and teacher are the same model, so that the per-token decrease is identical across candidates and any preference for length comes from the weight accounting:        

> weighted minus unweighted length ESS /N

> with the correction +0 .13 tokens 0.997

## Q RESAMPLING AND THE TRAINING WEIGHTS 

Resampling duplicates candidates. The training weights already recorded for earlier blocks are stored per candidate, so a duplicated candidate carries its ancestor’s recorded weight once per copy. Without a correction, the total training weight on an earlier block grows with every resampling step (a block that survives k steps is amplified by up to N k), and the opening tokens of every answer come to dominate the loss. Neither the loss value nor any standard diagnostic shows this. Duplication is how resampling represents weight, so the recorded training weights must be renor-malized to compensate. After each resampling step, we renormalize the stored training weights of every earlier block to sum to one across candidates. The test suite of Appendix O checks this property automatically. The sampler and the loss also keep separate weight accumulators. The sampler’s accumulator uses (2) and decides when to resample. Replacing it with the training weight, which depends on β,would stop the sampler from approximating πα. The loss accumulator uses (8), is reset at every block boundary, and supplies ¯A(i). Their per-token increments agree at β = 1 , but for every β they accumulate over different windows. We write the training weight with the letter A to keep it distinct from the importance weight W .
