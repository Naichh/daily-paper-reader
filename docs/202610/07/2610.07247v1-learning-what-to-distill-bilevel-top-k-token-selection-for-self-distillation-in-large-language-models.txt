# ABSTRACT 

Large language models have shown strong reasoning capabilities, but their high inference costs make knowledge distillation an important approach for transferring such capabilities to compact models in resource-constrained scenarios. On-policy self-distillation further reduces the reliance on external large teacher models while improving the reasoning ability of compact language models. However, existing methods typically either distill all token positions uniformly or select tokens using fixed heuristic criteria, assigning the same distillation strength to the selected positions rather than adaptively learning which tokens are most beneficial for distillation. To address these limitations, we propose BiToK-SD (Bi level To p-K

Token Selection for Self-Distillation), a bilevel-optimization-based token selection method that learns where distillation should be applied during on-policy self-distillation. Specifically, BiToK-SD is formulated as a bilevel optimization problem, where the lower-level problem models Top-K token selection as a differentiable threshold-based relaxation, allowing the selected positions to adapt as the student policy evolves, while the upper-level problem performs knowledge distillation on the selected positions. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that BiToK-SD achieves the best average performance among all compared methods while requiring only lightweight additional computation. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable progress on complex reasoning tasks (Wei et al., 2022). However, their large model sizes and high inference costs make them difficult to deploy in resource-constrained scenarios. Knowledge distillation (KD) provides an effective way to transfer the capabilities of stronger models to smaller ones, and has therefore been widely used to improve the reasoning ability of compact LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). Existing LLM distillation methods can be broadly categorized into off-policy and on-policy approaches: off-policy methods rely on fixed teacher outputs or pre-collected reasoning trajectories (Hsieh et al., 2023; Shridhar et al., 2023), while on-policy methods construct training signals from the student’s current rollouts to better match the student distribution (Agarwal et al., 2024). Despite their effectiveness, these methods often require either high-quality offline data, a strong external teacher model, or an online reward/teacher model, making the overall training process costly and less flexible. To reduce the dependence on external large teacher models, recent studies have begun to explore on-policy self-distillation (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026). Representative methods such as OPSD construct feedback-conditioned token-level correction signals from the model’s own generated reasoning trajectories, enabling the model to distill from itself (Zhao et al., 2026). Such 1arXiv:2610.07247v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 methods remove the need for a large teacher model and provide an efficient way to improve smaller reasoning models. However, they typically apply the same distillation objective uniformly across all token positions in the generated trajectory. This uniform treatment overlooks the heterogeneous roles of tokens in long chain-of-thought reasoning, where tokens may correspond to exploration, correction, reflection, or final answer derivation. A natural improvement is selective token-level distillation, where KD signals are applied only to token positions that are more important or more suitable for distillation. Recent studies have explored token selection or reweighting based on predefined indicators, such as token entropy, teacher–student divergence, reverse KL divergence, or their combinations such as Soft-OR scores (Tavor et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). However, because these methods rely on predefined token-level scores, their selection criteria remain fixed throughout training and are not adapted to the self-distillation objective. Since the student distribution changes continuously during on-policy self-distillation, fixed token-selection scores may fail to consistently identify token positions that are beneficial for distillation. Thus, the key question is not only whether token selection is needed, but how to learn adaptive token-level weights that determine both the selected positions and their distillation strengths. Learning token selection is challenging because Top-K selection is inherently discrete and non-differentiable. Prior work on X-risk minimization and learning to rank has shown that Top-K-related objectives often require specialized optimization techniques, including bilevel formulations and differentiable relaxations of ranking or sorting operations (Yang, 2022; Swezey et al., 2021). Inspired by this perspective, we formulate token selection as a learnable differentiable Top-K problem, enabling the selection mechanism to be optimized with respect to the self-distillation objective. To this end, we propose BiToK-SD, a bilevel-optimization-based Top-K token selection method for on-policy self-distillation. Unlike uniform distillation or heuristic token selection based on fixed importance scores, BiToK-SD learns adaptive token-level distillation weights through bilevel optimization, allowing the model to determine both where and how strongly distillation should be applied. We evaluate BiToK-SD on mathematical reasoning benchmarks under thinking-mode evaluation. Experimental results show that BiToK-SD achieves the best average performance among all compared methods while requiring only lightweight additional computation. Further analyses and ablation studies show that BiToK-SD preserves stronger policy exploration, induces more reflection-style reasoning behavior, and remains robust across different Top-K ratios. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Knowledge distillation (KD), following the classical teacher–student paradigm (Hinton et al., 2015) and its sequence-level extensions (Kim & Rush, 2016), has been widely used to transfer the reasoning and instruction-following abilities of large language models (LLMs) to smaller models (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Shing et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Existing KD methods can be categorized into off-policy and on-policy approaches. Off-policy KD relies on fixed teacher outputs, offline teacher-generated responses, or pre-collected reasoning trajectories (Hsieh et al., 2023; Shridhar et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023), which may cause distribution mismatch when the student is trained on static trajectories rather than its own rollouts. On-policy KD alleviates this issue by constructing supervision from student-generated trajectories, better matching the evolving student distribution (Shao et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025; 2026; Ko et al., 2026). More recently, self-distillation (SD) further reduces reliance on external teacher models by allowing the model to learn from its own generated reasoning trajectories (Chen et al., 2024; Hübotter et al., 2026; Sang et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026). For example, Zhao et al. (2026) propose OPSD, where a single model serves as both teacher and student during on-policy self-distillation. Since uniformly distilling all generated tokens may introduce redundant or even harmful supervision, recent work has begun to explore selective token-level distillation methods (Jung et al., 2025; Kim & Baek, 2026; Huang et al., 2025). Xu et al. (2026) propose Soft-OR scores to select informative tokens for distillation based on student uncertainty and teacher–student divergence. However, most existing selective distillation methods formulate token selection as a binary decision, assigning the same distillation weight to all selected positions without explicitly modeling their 2varying importance. This limitation suggests the need for more flexible token-level weighting mechanisms beyond binary selection. A related line of work studies differentiable optimization for ranking and top-K selection. Many ranking-oriented objectives, such as NDCG, MAP, and precision at top-K, depend on discrete ranks or top-K selection and are therefore difficult to optimize directly with gradient-based methods (Blondel et al., 2020; Yang, 2022). To address this issue, prior work has developed differentiable relaxations of sorting and ranking operators, such as PiRank (Swezey et al., 2021) and NeuralNDCG (Pobrotyn & Białobrzeski, 2021), as well as bilevel or compositional formulations for optimizing top-K ranking metrics such as NDCG (Qiu et al., 2022). While these methods provide useful tools for differentiable approximations of discrete top-K selection, they are designed for conventional ranking, retrieval, or recommendation tasks rather than adaptive token-level weighting in LLM self-distillation.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and Data. We conduct on-policy self-distillation experiments at three model scales, using Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B as base models. At each scale, all methods are initialized from the same checkpoint and trained on the same data to ensure a fair comparison. We train on the mathematical reasoning subset of OpenThoughts (Guha et al., 2025), which contains approximately 30K problem–solution pairs with chain-of-thought reasoning trajectories. 

Baselines. We compare BiToK-SD with some representative baselines covering supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, on-policy self-distillation, and heuristic token selection. SFT performs standard supervised fine-tuning on expert reasoning trajectories. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) optimizes the policy using binary outcome rewards and serves as a reinforcement-learning baseline. OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) performs on-policy self-distillation by constructing feedback-conditioned token-level correction signals from the model’s own rollouts. TIP (Xu et al., 2026) selects the top-50% positions by the same Soft-OR score (Eq. (2)) with a hard mask and, following the original method, optimizes the reverse KL divergence KL( pS ∥pT ) on the selected positions. 

Evaluation and Implementation. We evaluate on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 under thinking mode across three model scales (Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B). Since AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 each contain only a small number of problems, single-sample accuracy is subject to high variance. We therefore report Avg@64, the mean accuracy over 64 independently sampled solutions per problem. All methods are trained for 200 steps and evaluated at the final checkpoint; we deliberately avoid test-set-based checkpoint selection. 

Hyperparameters. We fine-tune all methods with AdamW using a learning rate of 5 × 10 −6, a per-device batch size of 2 with gradient accumulation of 2 over 4 GPUs (effective batch size 16 ), a maximum completion length of 1024 tokens, and a sampling temperature of 1.1. For the 8B model, we use a per-device batch size of 1 with gradient accumulation of 4 to fit 48GB GPUs, keeping the effective batch size at 16 . Parameter-efficient fine-tuning is performed with LoRA ( r = 64 , α = 128 )applied to all attention and MLP projections. For BiToK-SD, we adopt the Soft-OR score as the default token-level importance score and set the top-K ratio to k = 50% of generated tokens. For OPSD and BiToK-SD, we use the full-vocabulary teacher-to-student KL divergence as the token-level distillation objective. For the differentiable threshold relaxation, we set the softplus temperature to τ1 = 0 .1, the quadratic regularization coefficient to τ2 = 0 .01 , and the mask temperature to 

τm = 0 .1. We set ε = 10 −6 to avoid boundary ambiguity in Eq. (5) and δ = 10 −6 for numerical stability in Eq. (8). The smooth threshold ρ is updated by an inner threshold solver with step size 

η = 0 .1, momentum coefficient γ = 0 .9, and variance-reduction coefficient βρ = 0 .1.Unless otherwise stated, all baselines share the same optimizer, learning rate, effective batch size, number of training steps, LoRA configuration, and evaluation decoding settings as BiToK-SD. For GRPO, we use a group size of G = 8 rollouts per prompt (two prompts per rollout batch), a maximum completion length of 8,192 tokens, a sampling temperature of 1.2, two optimization iterations per rollout batch, and no KL penalty, so that GRPO processes 200 distinct prompts over 200 steps. These results suggest that the benefit of BiToK-SD does not come from simply increasing the amount of distillation or uniformly enforcing teacher imitation, but from selectively applying token-level supervision to positions that are more useful for reasoning-oriented self-distillation. These dynamics show that the relaxed threshold adapts throughout training rather than remaining effectively fixed. 4.4 GENERALIZATION TO OFF -P OLICY KD To assess the generality of our bilevel token-selection framework, we further evaluate it under the off-policy distillation setting. Following the protocol of Tavor et al. (2026), we distill Qwen3-8B into Qwen3-1.7B on an 80M-token subset of FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024), with a maximum sequence length of 512. Training runs for one epoch with an effective batch size of 64, using a micro-batch size of 2 and gradient accumulation of 8 on each of 4 GPUs, yielding roughly 2.4K optimizer updates. We use a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5, a KD temperature of τ = 1 .0, and disable the cross-entropy term (αce = 0 ) so that all gradients originate from the distillation objective. To eliminate teacher forward passes during training, we precompute and cache, for every position, the teacher’s top-64 vocabulary probabilities together with its entropy, which is required for the truncated distillation objective. We compare against Full-KD, which distills all token positions; SE-KD, which selects token positions based on student entropy; and SE-KD 3X , which extends selective distillation beyond the position axis by additionally applying selection along the class and sample axes, following Tavor et al. (2026). A practical constraint of this off-policy setup is that storing the teacher’s full output distribution for every token position is prohibitively expensive. Therefore, only a truncated top-U slice, with U = 64 ,is retained on disk. As a result, statistics that require access to the complete teacher distribution— most notably the forward and reverse KL terms used by the Soft-OR importance score in our main experiments—cannot be evaluated faithfully. We therefore replace Soft-OR with the student-entropy score, which is computed solely from the student’s predictions and is independent of the granularity of the cached teacher distribution. For the bilevel selector, we use the same relaxation hyperparameters as in the main experiments ( τ1 = 0 .1, τ2 = 0 .01 , τm = 0 .1), and fix the position-selection ratio at 

k = 20% .We evaluate on five downstream tasks—ARC-Easy, GSM8K, HellaSwag, PIQA, and LAMBADA— and report the average accuracy (Avg. Acc.) over these five tasks, the strict instruction-following score on the IFEval benchmark, and the perplexity (PPL) on LAMBADA. As shown in Table 3, BiToK-SD outperforms Full-KD, SE-KD, and SE-KD 3X across all three summary metrics. These results suggest that the proposed bilevel selection mechanism can also be applied to off-policy KD, even when only truncated teacher distributions are available. 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced BiToK-SD, a bilevel Top-K token-selection framework for on-policy self-distillation in large language models. Instead of distilling all generated tokens uniformly or relying on fixed heuristic selection rules, BiToK-SD learns adaptive token-level distillation weights through a differentiable threshold-based optimization formulation. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that BiToK-SD consistently improves over strong baselines while requiring only lightweight 9additional computation. Further analyses indicate that the learned selector helps preserve policy exploration and is associated with more reflection-style reasoning behavior. Additional ablation and off-policy KD experiments further demonstrate the robustness and generality of the proposed selection mechanism. Overall, our results suggest that learning where and how strongly to apply distillation is a promising direction for improving self-distillation in reasoning-oriented language models.
