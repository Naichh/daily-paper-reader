ABSTRACT
Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) usually optimizes an-
swer correctness, yet useful language-model behavior also requires high-quality
reasoning and concise responses. Existing multi-reward post-training methods
typically scalarize rewards or combine specialists without explicitly protecting a
reward priority order. This is problematic when trade-offs are asymmetric: con-
ciseness, for example, should not improve at the cost of correctness. We introduce
Lexicographic Multi-Objective On-Policy Distillation (LMOPD), a multi-teacher
method for integrating reward-specialized policies under explicit priorities. For
each student rollout, LMOPD selects the specialist for the first objective whose
gate detects a deficiency, then locally projects its centered log-policy correction
to remove components that oppose higher-priority specialists. We evaluate 30B-
A3B mixture-of-experts transformer models in two- and four-expert settings on
three math benchmarks, measuring retained specialist gains. With two experts,
LMOPD’s point estimates fully retain the accuracy and reasoning-quality gains
while acquiring 46.9% of the conciseness gain.
With four experts, it retains
≈90% of both the accuracy gain and reasoning-correctness gain, compared to
only ≈57% by the next best evaluated baseline. Matched four-expert ablations
show that lexicographic routing outperforms random routing and that projection
further strengthens both top-priority capabilities. Across both scales, LMOPD
preserves the highest-priority capabilities more effectively than the existing base-
lines we evaluate, demonstrating the value of explicit priorities for specialist inte-
gration.

INTRODUCTION
Post-training has become a critical stage in building large language models (LLMs) that can solve
difficult reasoning and agentic tasks. Policy-gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024)
enable models to improve from outcome-level feedback, and reinforcement learning from verifiable
rewards (RLVR) has been especially effective in domains such as mathematics and code where cor-
rectness can be automatically verified. Modern reasoning systems consequently devote substantial
post-training compute to reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks (Yang et al., 2025).
Outcome correctness, however, is only one dimension of response quality. A useful mathematical
assistant should also reason correctly, verify its work, avoid unnecessary dead ends, and commu-
nicate concisely. These objectives can conflict and have reward signals with different scales and
densities. A standard solution is scalarization; GDPO, for example, normalizes reward dimensions
separately before combining them (Liu et al., 2026a). A fixed scalarization still treats objectives as
compensatory: enough improvement on a lower-priority axis can offset degradation of a prerequi-
site. Many applications instead require a non-compensatory hierarchy in which presentation quality
cannot redeem an incorrect solution.
An alternative is to specialize separate models and combine them after training. Rewarded Soups
(Ram´e et al., 2023), for example, interpolates parameters of models optimized for different rewards.

On-policy distillation (OPD) instead transfers dense teacher feedback on student-generated trajec-
tories (Agarwal et al., 2024). Multi-teacher OPD (MOPD) routes rollouts to domain-specialized
teachers (Ma et al., 2026); there, the prompt domain determines which teacher rollouts are routed
to. We study when several objectives apply to the same prompt, where the missing signal is instead
which requirement the individual sampled solution fails.
We propose Lexicographic Multi-Objective On-Policy Distillation (LMOPD) for priority-
ordered specialist integration. LMOPD uses each rollout’s reward profile to make the routing de-
cision. We assume an ordered set of objectives r1 ≻r2 ≻· · · ≻rK and a collection of reward-
specialized experts. For each on-policy student rollout, LMOPD walks through the rewards in pri-
ority order. A discrete objective routes to its expert when its maximum acceptable reward has not
been achieved; a continuous objective routes when the rollout has negative group-relative advantage
on that objective. The first objective that triggers determines the teacher. If every objective passes,
the rollout is routed to the student itself (effectively contributing no gradient) to avoid unnecessarily
perturbing already correct behavior. Higher-priority objectives therefore have the first opportunity
to correct a trajectory, while lower-priority experts only act after higher-priority requirements are
satisfied.
Routing alone does not prevent a selected lower-priority expert from proposing a token-level correc-
tion that opposes a higher-priority expert. We therefore represent each expert’s correction as a cen-
tered log-policy delta and sequentially remove components with negative inner product against the
higher-priority expert deltas. This projection is performed in function space rather than parameter-
gradient space and is implemented through streamed per-token Gram matrices, avoiding one addi-
tional student backward pass per expert.
This paper makes three contributions:
2


• We formulate lexicographic corrective routing for multi-teacher OPD. The per-rollout rule
handles absolute discrete criteria and relative continuous rewards, allows several objectives
to share one expert, and self-distills when no correction is required.
• We introduce a one-sided function-space projection that removes components of the routed
correction that oppose higher-priority experts, together with a streamed Gram-space imple-
mentation that requires no extra student backward passes.
• We evaluate LMOPD in two- and four-expert settings at 30B scale and isolate routing and
projection through component ablations.
We evaluate whether LMOPD retains prerequisite capabilities, improves teacher assignment over
random routing, and redirects integration when projection activates. LMOPD is a local supervision
heuristic, not a claim of globally optimal lexicographic control.

RELATED WORK
RLVR and multi-reward reinforcement learning.
PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) and GRPO
(Shao et al., 2024) are widely used policy-gradient methods for LLM post-training. RLVR cou-
ples these updates with automatically verifiable outcome rewards, particularly for mathematical and
coding tasks. Multi-reward extensions introduce additional reward dimensions for properties such
as style, format, length, or process quality. GDPO (Liu et al., 2026a) identifies a failure mode in
naively applying group normalization after summing heterogeneous rewards and instead normalizes
each reward dimension independently before aggregation. This preserves reward-specific variation,
but the final policy update still depends on a scalarized combination of objectives. Recent work also
uses conditional reward structures. SpatialThinker explicitly uses lexicographically gated rewards
in multimodal RL (Batra et al., 2025), while SCOPE-RL gates process-quality rewards on final-
answer correctness in mathematical reasoning (Liu et al., 2026b). LMOPD shares the intuition that
lower-priority feedback should not override task success, but uses this hierarchy to select a teacher
distribution rather than to construct a scalar reward.
Combining reward-specialized policies.
Rewarded Soups (Ram´e et al., 2023) trains policies on
different rewards from a shared initialization, linearly interpolates their weights, and uses the result-
ing merged model for all inputs and trajectories.
On-policy and multi-teacher distillation.
Knowledge distillation trains a student to match a
teacher distribution; reverse KL provides a mode-seeking signal for autoregressive LLMs (Gu et al.,
2024). GKD uses student-generated sequences (Agarwal et al., 2024) instead, providing feedback
that is both dense and on-policy. MOPD extends GKD by dispatching rollouts to domain-specialized
teachers to mix capabilities across domains and multiple teachers(Ma et al., 2026). MOPD has
emerged as a key component of frontier LLM post-training recipes, including MiMo-V2-Flash Xiao
et al. (2026), DeepSeek-V4 Xu et al. (2026), GLM-5 Zeng et al. (2026), and Nemotron-Cascade
2 Yang et al. (2026). SMOPD, a concurrent work, first trains reward specialists, then merges a
token-level mixture of all teachers with a balanced multi-reward policy-gradient anchor (Wang et al.,
2026).
At the mechanism level, LMOPD is related to PCGrad, which removes pairwise conflicting
parameter-gradient components in multi-task learning (Yu et al., 2020). LMOPD instead filters one
routed teacher correction only against earlier priorities, using an unweighted token-level function-
space surrogate rather than parameter gradients.
Lexicographic optimization.
Lexicographic multi-objective reinforcement learning optimizes
objectives according to an ordered preference relation rather than treating all trade-offs as inter-
changeable. Recent work studies this setting theoretically and algorithmically, including regret
guarantees for linear MDPs (Xue et al., 2025) and thresholded lexicographic formulations (Tercan
& Prabhu, 2024). LMOPD does not attempt to provide the same policy-level optimality guarantees.
Instead, it borrows the priority structure to select a corrective teacher signal and locally project that
signal during distillation.
3


Across these lines of work, LMOPD differs in three coupled choices: unlike domain-routed MOPD,
it routes individual rollouts by reward deficiency; unlike Rewarded Soups and SMOPD, it does not
globally or simultaneously merge all specialists; and unlike GDPO, it does not collapse priorities
into a scalarized advantage.
3
METHOD
LMOPD has two conceptually separable components.
First, lexicographic rollout routing uses
the reward profile of each student-generated completion to select the specialist associated with
its highest-priority deficiency. Second, priority-aware projection modifies the selected specialist’s
token-level correction whenever it opposes a higher-priority specialist. This decomposition yields
a routing-only method and a routing-plus-projection method with the same rollout assignments and
distillation loss. Figure 1 summarizes the complete pipeline.

ON-POLICY DISTILLATION
At the start of an update, we freeze an anchor ¯πθ ←πθ, sample student rollouts, and minimize a
token-averaged reverse KL toward a fixed target distribution together with a hard-label cross entropy
term (Agarwal et al., 2024; Hinton et al., 2015). The target is the routed specialist, the anchor itself,
or the projected virtual teacher introduced below. Unlike domain-based MOPD (Ma et al., 2026),
LMOPD chooses this target from the rollout’s reward profile. Appendix A.1 gives the complete
objective and notation.

3.4
STAGE II: PRIORITY-AWARE FUNCTION-SPACE PROJECTION
Why routing alone is insufficient.
Routing controls which expert supervises a rollout, but it does
not control the direction of that expert’s update. A lower-priority expert can therefore assign a
correction that locally opposes a higher-priority capability. LMOPD tests compatibility with an inner
product between token-level corrections. The test concerns only the teacher-dependent reverse-KL
component and excludes the hard-label cross-entropy term. This surrogate avoids an additional
student backward pass per expert compared to computing the inner product on the actual gradients;
more details can be found in Appendix A.3.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
TRAINING
Datasets.
We use a proprietary mathematical-reasoning corpus for post-training.
5


Reward functions.
We use binary answer correctness, reasoning quality judged by a 31B Gemma
4 judge (Team et al., 2026) as an average over four axes (reasoning correctness, verification, clarity,
and efficiency), and a bounded length-based score Sconc. All rewards are in the interval [0, 1]. Every
reported Conc metric is computed from raw Sconc. The conciseness specialist is trained with a
correctness-gated version where it only receives Sconc on correct rollouts and 0 otherwise, whereas
GDPO directly optimizes raw Sconc. Appendix C.5 gives the complete definitions.
The orders encode prerequisites rather than interchangeable utilities. Correctness comes first be-
cause presentation cannot redeem a wrong answer; reasoning correctness follows as a core capa-
bility, then verification, efficiency, and conciseness refine otherwise correct solutions. We therefore
read results from highest to lowest priority and treat their macro-average as a descriptive summary.
Expert models.
We train reward-specialized experts from the shared base and select teachers by
target-reward validation performance. This produces the two integration settings below; See Ap-
pendix C.6 for further details on specialist training and expert selection.
Two-expert setting (three rewards).
The first setting asks whether a student can acquire a lower-priority behavior from one specialist
while retaining the core mathematical capability of another. We order the objectives as RC ≻
RRQ ≻Rconc and use two teachers:
1. a capability expert, selected from the reasoning-specialized runs and used for both the
correctness and aggregate reasoning-quality gates; and
2. a conciseness expert, used for the conciseness gate.
Because both higher-priority gates share one capability teacher, this setting isolates adding concise-
ness while retaining core mathematical capability. As we use one teacher for both RC and RRQ,
rollouts that route to either one of those gates would get the same loss.
Four-expert setting (five rewards).
The second setting tests a longer priority chain with supervision distributed across more specialists.
We use RC ≻RRC ≻RRV ≻RRE ≻Rconc. The four teachers are:
1. a reasoning-correctness expert, shared by the correctness and reasoning-correctness gates;
2. a reasoning-verification expert;
3. a reasoning-efficiency expert; and
4. a conciseness expert.
This setting tests integration under a longer chain in which distinct specialist corrections compete
and later teachers act only after earlier gates pass. It therefore tests both multi-expert capability
integration and the scaling behavior of the lexicographic routing rule.
Student model.
The LMOPD student and all RL-trained specialist and baseline policies start from
the same roughly 30B-parameter mixture-of-experts checkpoint, with approximately 3B active pa-
rameters per token. The checkpoint is an internal predecessor of Cohere’s North Mini Code (Team
Cohere, 2026).
Baselines.
We compare LMOPD with baselines that isolate different components of the method.
Rewarded Soup. Rewarded Soups (Ram´e et al., 2023) linearly interpolates the parameters of reward-
specialized policies trained from the same initialization. We evaluate both a uniform interpolation
and a correctness-emphasized interpolation selected without using held-out test sets. This tests
whether capability integration can be achieved by a static weight-space merge rather than by on-
policy teacher supervision.
GDPO. GDPO (Liu et al., 2026a) is our primary multi-objective RL baseline. It optimizes raw
correctness, aggregate reasoning quality, and raw Sconc, normalizing each reward dimension before
combining the advantages into one policy-gradient direction. GDPO uses objective weights, rather
6


than hand-designed gates, to control the trade-off. We evaluate equal weights and correctness-
emphasized 5:1 and 25:1 weightings; the main tables show equal weights and the best-tuned 25:1
run, while Table 4 retains the intermediate configuration.
Correctness-Gated RLVR. As a lexicographic reward-design control, Correctness-Gated RLVR
makes correctness a prerequisite by zeroing the aggregate reasoning-quality and conciseness re-
wards on incorrect rollouts, then scalarizes the rewards that remain. Appendix C.7 gives both base-
line objectives. Because RRQ is the mean of the same four reasoning-quality axes, Correctness-
Gated RLVR and each GDPO weighting receive the same three aggregate rewards in the two- and
four-expert settings. We therefore reuse the same step-300 policy checkpoints in both tables; the
four-expert table only decomposes aggregate reasoning quality into component axes for evaluation.
Individual specialists. We report the frozen reward-specialized experts themselves. These models
are not unified multi-objective solutions; instead, they establish the per-objective headroom available
from specialization and define the reference point for retained gain.
Component ablations. The routing-only ablation is exactly the same as LMOPD but skips the pro-
jection step. This isolates the contribution of priority-aware projection. We also present a random-
routing control which is exactly the same as the routing-only variant but matches experts to rollouts
at random. This tests whether improvements come merely from exposure to multiple teachers or
from matching teacher identity to the rollout’s reward deficiencies and priority order.
Training settings.
Two- and four-expert LMOPD variants train for 40 and 80 steps, respectively,
and use their final checkpoints. All RL-trained specialist and baseline policies use their final step-
300 checkpoint. See Appendix C for further details.

5.2
FOUR-EXPERT RETAINED GAIN
The four-expert setting tests whether high-priority capabilities remain protected as the priority chain
grows to five objectives. Table 2 shows that LMOPD retains (90.6 ± 15.0)% of the accuracy gain
(raw: 0.711 →0.748) and (88.6 ± 7.0)% of the reasoning-correctness gain (raw: 0.755 →0.805).
Every other unified method reaches at most 57.1% and 63.5%, respectively. LMOPD also has the
highest macro-average point estimate (39.6%, versus 34.4% for Correctness-Gated RLVR).
Because the objectives are lexicographically ordered, the columns should be read in priority order
rather than as equally consequential targets. The central scaling result is therefore LMOPD’s large
margin on accuracy and reasoning correctness, followed by a positive composite-conciseness gain
(28.0 ± 1.1%). Reasoning verification and efficiency are lower-priority objectives: their retained
gains of −6.8% and −2.4% do not outweigh preservation of the first two objectives under the stated
preference order. This profile is consistent with the intended behavior of LMOPD—preserving
high-priority capabilities even when all specialist directions cannot be improved simultaneously.
LMOPD outperforms other multi-objective optimization baselines on important objectives.

LIMITATIONS
LMOPD serves as a local heuristic rather than global lexicographic optimality: its function-space
test approximates parameter-gradient compatibility, and one sweep may leave residual conflicts.
Strict routing can starve later experts (Appendix A.4), and outcomes ultimately depend on reward
and teacher quality. We evaluate only proprietary-model mathematical reasoning; open-weight and
cross-domain validation remain future work. Because 30B-scale post-training is costly, multi-seed
replication is limited to two-expert LMOPD: three 40 step runs (see Appendix C.4). The main-table
run is the median seed by average retained gain (84.4%; the other seeds obtain 81.2% and 95.1%);
Table 5 and fig. 2 give the complete results. Except for this replication study, reported SEs quantify
evaluation sampling, not training-run variation.

CONCLUSION
We introduced LMOPD, which routes each rollout to the specialist for its highest-priority unmet ob-
jective and projects that specialist’s token-level correction against earlier experts. With two experts,
LMOPD retains specialist-level accuracy and aggregate reasoning quality while acquiring concise-
ness; with four, it retains 90.6% of the accuracy gain and 88.6% of the gain in reasoning correctness,
with higher point estimates than multi-objective baselines on both top priorities. Ablations show that
deficiency-aware routing is essential and that, when materially engaged with four experts, projection
shifts point estimates toward these priorities. Together, these results position rollout-conditioned dis-
tillation as a practical path to integrating specialists without collapsing prioritized capabilities into a
single global trade-off.

A.2
ROUTING CONSTRAINT CONSTRUCTION
Routing intuition.
The priority ordering gives each objective a “first right of refusal” over the
rollout. With correctness first, an incorrect response is always corrected by the capability expert
and cannot be pushed toward conciseness merely because it is short. Once correctness passes, the
next objective is allowed to inspect the rollout, and so on. Continuous objectives use group-relative
advantage so that the method asks whether a rollout is unusually weak on that dimension compared
with alternative completions of the same prompt. Self-distillation prevents trajectories that already
satisfy every gate from being arbitrarily pulled toward a specialist. The resulting update is still dense
at the token level; only the identity of the teacher is selected lexicographically.

One sweep versus exact feasibility.
With at most one active constraint, the update is the exact
orthogonal projection onto the corresponding half-space, up to ϵ and numerical guards. With two or
more non-orthogonal constraints, a later step can reintroduce a negative inner product with an earlier
expert. Repeated sweeps converge toward the intersection of the half-spaces, but our implementation
uses one sweep by default because most routed rows have zero or one constraint.
Efficient implementation.
Because the projected direction remains in the span of the expert cor-
rections, we implement the same function-space sweep in coefficient space using streamed per-token
M ×M Gram matrices. This avoids both vocabulary-sized correction tensors and additional student
backward passes. Appendix B gives the algebraic construction, streaming procedure, and numerical
safeguards.

C
EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
C.1
TRAINING PROTOCOL
We study LMOPD on mathematical reasoning using a proprietary internal training set. All poli-
cies start from the same post-SFT checkpoint: a roughly 30B-parameter mixture-of-experts model
with approximately 3B active parameters per token. The checkpoint is an internal predecessor of
Cohere’s North Mini Code (Team Cohere, 2026). The checkpoint has completed general pretrain-
ing and supervised post-training on multiple domains, including mathematical reasoning, code, and
instruction following. We train and evaluate with a maximum context length of 32K tokens.
Because LMOPD distillation dynamics differ from RLVR and with the number of experts to be
distilled from, we run 40 training steps for all 2 expert LMOPD variants, and 80 steps for 4 expert
LMOPD variants. Each step uses 64 prompts with 8 freshly sampled rollouts per prompt, giving
512 fully on-policy trajectories per update. We only filter out empty responses. We use a two-
stage learning-rate schedule: 4 × 10−6 for the first 40 steps and 4 × 10−7 thereafter. All reward-
specialist and direct multi-reward RL runs use a common 300-step horizon and their final step-300
checkpoints, with additional filters for zero-variance groups.
C.2
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Our final evaluation suite contains AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT February 2025. AIME
is a standard short-answer competition benchmark used extensively in recent LLM reasoning re-
ports, including Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025). HMMT February 2025 provides a distinct set of recent
competition problems from the Harvard–MIT Mathematics Tournament (Harvard-MIT Mathematics
Tournament, 2025) and is included in the MathArena evaluation ecosystem (Balunovi´c et al., 2025).
None of these benchmarks is used for training or checkpoint selection. We evaluate each problem
with 50 stochastic rollouts at temperature 0.6. Repeated rollouts reduce Monte Carlo noise for each
problem but do not create additional independent benchmark items, so uncertainty estimates and sig-
nificance tests treat problems, rather than individual sampled completions, as the primary evaluation
units. We note that public competition benchmarks can be vulnerable to pretraining contamination;
MathArena reports evidence of this issue for AIME 2024 (Balunovi´c et al., 2025), motivating the
inclusion of the more recent HMMT set and appropriately scoped claims.

C.5
REWARD DEFINITIONS
We use one verifiable outcome reward, four process-quality axes, and a length-based conciseness
reward.
Correctness.
RC(x, y) ∈{0, 1} indicates whether the model’s final answer exactly matches the
gold answer after answer normalization.
Reasoning quality.
A frozen 31B Gemma 4 judge model (Team et al., 2026) evaluates four axes:
reasoning correctness (whether the derivation steps are valid), verification (whether the response
checks or validates its answer), clarity (whether the reasoning is understandable and well organized),
and efficiency (whether the reasoning avoids unnecessary dead ends). The full prompt for the judge
is included in section E For each axis a, the judge emits a penalty
pa ∈{0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.0},
Ra = 1 −pa,
(25)
where larger reward is better. We define aggregate reasoning quality as
RRQ = 1
4 (RRC + RRV + RRCl + RRE) ,
(26)
for reasoning correctness, verification, clarity, and efficiency, respectively.
Conciseness.
Let L(y) be the number of completion tokens. We first define an intrinsic concise-
ness score
Sconc(y) = 1 −λ
L(y)
KL + L(y),
(27)
with KL = 16,384 and λ = 0.5. This bounded penalty discourages unnecessary length without
making long but correct solutions arbitrarily poor. All reported Conc metrics evaluate Sconc directly,
independent of correctness. The conciseness specialist is instead trained with the correctness-gated
reward to mitigate reward hacking:
Rconc(x, y) = RC(x, y) Sconc(y),
(28)
so an incorrect short answer cannot obtain a high specialist-training reward. GDPO is the exception
during training: its conciseness dimension is the raw Sconc score as the objective weights should
serve to mitigate reward hacking.

C.6
SPECIALIST TRAINING AND SELECTION
We train candidate specialists from the same base checkpoint for 300 RL steps, separately targeting
correctness, each of the four reasoning-quality subaxes, aggregate reasoning quality, and concise-
ness. This specialization stage intentionally isolates objectives before any capability integration is
attempted. We retain specialists based on their target-reward validation performance rather than
assuming one usable expert per reward. In pilot specialist training, the specialists for aggregate rea-
soning quality and reasoning correctness are stronger capability teachers than the binary-correctness
specialist, consistent with the denser feedback supplied by the process reward. We therefore allow
these experts to serve the correctness gate as well. We omit a separate clarity expert from the integra-
tion experiments because specialist training does not provide a meaningful benchmark improvement
on clarity over the starting model.
