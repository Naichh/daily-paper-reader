## ABSTRACT 

On-policy distillation (OPD) improves the reasoning capabilities of small lan-guage models through token-level teacher supervision on student-generated tra-jectories. Yet can teachers that excel at solving problems independently also guide student reasoning effectively? Prior work shows that when student prefixes fol-low reasoning paths that differ from the teacher’s own or contain errors, teachers can be less accurate when continuing from these prefixes than when solving prob-lems independently. To this end, we propose Prep-OPD, which uses reinforcement learning (RL) before distillation to train the teacher to adapt to the student’s ex-isting reasoning state and correct course when errors arise. Training optimizes teacher continuations from fixed student prefixes using final-answer correctness as the reward. The prepared teacher then trains the student through trajectory guidance and token-level supervision. We evaluate Prep-OPD on eight mathe-matical reasoning benchmarks, using Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as the teacher and Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B as students. With the 4B teacher and 1.7B student, Prep-OPD improves average accuracy over standard OPD and the strongest base-line, Relay-OPD, by 8.28 and 2.30 percentage points, respectively. Controlled experiments further show that teacher RL conditioned on student-generated pre-fixes yields higher student accuracy than problem-start teacher RL with and with-out handoff on Qwen3-1.7B. Reusing the same prepared teacher also improves Qwen3-0.6B. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

On-policy distillation (OPD) is a promising approach for transferring reasoning capabilities from strong language models to smaller students. The student generates its own trajectories, while the teacher provides dense, token-level supervision on student-generated prefixes, often through reverse KL minimization (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Training on student-generated trajectories helps reduce the distribution mismatch between the prefixes seen during train-ing and those produced at inference (Lin et al., 2020). OPD can also achieve competitive reasoning performance at substantially lower training cost than a reinforcement learning (RL) baseline (Zhao et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026). However, OPD implicitly relies on the teacher being a reliable supervisor on student-generated reasoning states. This assumption can break down when student prefixes follow reasoning paths dif-ferent from those the teacher would take independently, or have already gone in the wrong direction. In such cases, the teacher can be substantially less accurate when continuing from these prefixes than when solving the same problems from scratch. Its token-level feedback may also be noisy or even misleading (Koo et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026b). Existing work improves teacher guidance in several ways. Veto stabilizes optimization through adaptive distillation targets, but remains constrained by the teacher’s existing continuation capabili-ties (Jang et al., 2026). ESR and FastOPD concentrate supervision on early prefixes where teacher guidance is more effective, but exclude reasoning steps beyond the truncation point from distillation supervision for that training instance (Zhou et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). TRD improves distil-lation samples by rewriting complete student trajectories, whereas SKD and Relay-OPD introduce teacher-generated tokens or segments during rollouts to intervene in student trajectories (Jiang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025; 2026). However, even with early intervention, teacher continuations may still be influenced by errors in the student’s reasoning. These methods do not explicitly train the teacher to adapt to the student’s existing reasoning state and correct course when errors arise. This suggests a simple but different strategy: prepare the teacher for student-generated reasoning states before using it for distillation . To this end, we propose Prep-OPD, which uses reinforcement learning to train the teacher to adapt to the student’s existing reasoning state and correct course when errors arise. We then distill this ability into the student. We sample prefixes at different reasoning stages from trajectories generated by the initial student. The teacher continues reasoning while pre-serving each prefix and is trained through reinforcement learning with final-answer correctness as the reward, yielding the prepared teacher TP . We then freeze and reuse the same TP : it supplies bounded continuations at selected intermediate states in fresh student rollouts and provides token-level distillation supervision over the entire trajectory jointly generated by the student and teacher. The teacher’s continuations both provide context for the student’s subsequent reasoning and con-tribute directly to its updates, allowing the student to learn to build on existing reasoning and adjust its direction when needed. ## 2 TEACHER SUPERVISION ON STUDENT TRAJECTORIES 

On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on trajectories sampled from its own policy, while a fixed teacher provides token-level supervision (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026). Let Sθ denote the student, S¯θ its rollout policy, and T the teacher. Given a problem x, the student generates 

y = ( y1, . . . , y N ) ∼ S¯θ (· | x), ht = ( x, y <t ). (1) Standard OPD typically employs the reverse KL divergence. Taking a single-sample estimate at the student-sampled token yt, the teacher–student log-probability difference defines the token-level advantage 

bAOPD 

t = log T (yt | ht) − log S¯θ (yt | ht). (2) A positive advantage increases the probability of the sampled token, whereas a negative one de-creases it. By conditioning supervision on ht, OPD grounds learning in the reasoning states actually visited by the student rather than exclusively in teacher-generated trajectories. However, student-generated reasoning may contain errors or commit to unproductive directions, which can compromise subsequent teacher supervision (Fu et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026b). In such cases, the teacher must provide useful guidance while inheriting the reasoning already produced by the student, rather than solving the problem again from a clean starting point. A teacher’s ability to solve problems from scratch therefore does not, by itself, establish its ability to provide effective supervision under student-generated reasoning. This motivates explicitly preparing the teacher to continue toward correct solutions from student-induced reasoning states. 

## 3 PREP -OPD 

To address this requirement, we propose Prep-OPD , a two-stage framework that first uses rein-forcement learning to train the teacher to adapt to the student’s existing reasoning state and correct course when errors arise, and then reuses the same prepared teacher to guide and supervise student learning. In Stage 1 , we condition the teacher on student-generated reasoning prefixes and optimize only its continuations, using final-answer correctness as the reward, yielding the prepared teacher 

TP (Sections 3.1–3.2). In Stage 2 , we freeze and reuse the same TP to guide student trajectories through bounded continuations and provide token-level distillation supervision over the entire tra-jectory jointly generated by the student and teacher (Section 3.3). Figure 2 illustrates the overall framework. 3Preprint 3.1 STUDENT -C ONDITIONED CONTINUATION TASKS 

To prepare the teacher to adapt to reasoning states actually generated by the student, we extract unfinished reasoning prefixes from trajectories of a fixed initial student S0 to construct student-conditioned continuation tasks. Let D denote a dataset of problems x paired with reference answers 

a. For each problem, we first sample a student response and then retain an unfinished prefix corre-sponding to an intermediate reasoning state: 

(x, a ) ∼ D , y ∼ S0(· | x), k ∼ κ(· | y), p = y1: k, (3) where κ is a cut-point distribution over valid intermediate positions. Repeating this procedure yields a fixed empirical distribution q0 over continuation tasks (x, p, a ).Each task asks the teacher to continue from the inherited student reasoning p, rather than solve the problem again from a clean starting point. The reference answer a is not exposed to the teacher and is used only to evaluate the resulting continuation. Importantly, p remains fixed throughout the task: the teacher cannot delete or rewrite reasoning already produced by the student, and any adjustment must therefore be expressed through subsequent tokens. Sampling cut points at different depths exposes the teacher to student reasoning states from different stages of a trajectory, ranging from relatively early partial solutions to more developed reasoning contexts. 3.2 TEACHER PREPARATION VIA REINFORCEMENT LEARNING

z ∼ Tϕ(· | x, p ), (4) where z denotes the teacher-generated continuation. The completed response is p ⊕ z, and we optimize

Having learned to continue from inherited student reasoning, the frozen TP is reused in Stage 2 in two complementary roles: at selected states, it contributes continuations that steer the student rollout toward more reliable reasoning directions; over the resulting mixed trajectory, it provides token-level supervision. At each handoff, the prefix of the current mixed trajectory plays the same role as p in Stage 1: it is preserved as inherited reasoning, while TP determines what comes next. Following Relay-OPD (Xu et al., 2026), we use bounded teacher handoffs to insert continuations at selected prefixes of the mixed trajectory. During a student segment, while the handoff budget remains, TP takes over when its most likely next token is a reflection token (Muennighoff et al., 2025) and no reflection token appears among the rollout student S¯θ ’s top-5 next-token candidates at the same prefix. Equation 14 in Appendix A.2 specifies this criterion; the same appendix defines the reflection-token set and handoff budgets. At a handoff, TP emits the preferred reflection token and then samples a bounded continuation. After this segment, control returns to the student unless the rollout has terminated. The resulting mixed trajectory τ = ( τ1, . . . , τ N ) contains both student- and teacher-generated tokens. The same frozen TP then provides OPD supervision over the mixed trajectory. For hτt = ( x, τ <t ),we define 

bAPt = sg[log TP (τt | hτt ) − log S¯θ (τt | hτt )] , (9) where sg denotes stop-gradient. We optimize all realized response tokens with a clipped sampled-token distillation surrogate; the complete student update, token averaging, and rollout budgets are specified in Appendix A. Teacher-generated continuations therefore both shape the contexts from which the student resumes generation and participate directly in the student update, while the same TP provides dense token-level supervision over the full mixed trajectory. After distillation, TP is removed and inference uses the student alone. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and datasets. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as the initial teacher T0 and Qwen3-1.7B as the primary student S0, with an additional Qwen3-0.6B setting (Yang et al., 2025). Training problems come from the English subset of DAPO-Math-17K (Yu et al., 2025). Both student dis-tillation settings use 16 ,164 problems, while the GRPO stage of teacher preparation uses a 2,048 -problem training subset. We evaluate on the eight mathematical reasoning benchmarks used by Xu et al. (2026): AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AIME 2026, AMC 2023, HMMT February 2026, HMMT November 2025, OlympiadBench, and MATH-500. 

Baselines. We include the initial student as a reference and compare with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), Standard OPD (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024), TRD (Jiang et al., 2026), IW-OPD (Xie et al., 2026b), FastOPD (Zhang et al., 2026), and Relay-OPD (Xu et al., 2026). In the main Qwen3-1.7B comparison, the distillation baselines use TB , obtained by problem-start RL from T0,whereas Prep-OPD uses TP , trained on prefixes from S0. The two RL-trained teachers use the same training-problem pool and outcome verifier. For Qwen3-0.6B, we use the original teacher T0

for TRD because it yielded higher student accuracy than the problem-start RL teacher TB in this full-trajectory rewriting setting. The other distillation baselines in Table 1 use TB . Relay-OPD comparisons using T0 at both student sizes are reported separately in Appendix A.6. 

Implementation details. We implement training with verl and vLLM on H20 GPUs, with thinking disabled for both students. Teacher preparation runs for 128 GRPO steps; student distillation at both sizes uses one epoch. We use a 16 ,384 -token generation cap throughout training and evaluation; FastOPD truncates its training rollouts at 4,096 tokens. Training and evaluation use temperature 1.0

and top-p 1.0. For each training configuration, we select the student checkpoint with the highest accuracy on a development set. ## 5 RELATED WORK 

On-policy distillation. On-policy distillation (OPD) builds on knowledge distillation, which transfers teacher distributions or generated sequences to a student (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016). For LLM post-training, OPD evaluates teacher predictions along student-generated rollouts, combining on-policy sampling with dense supervision through token-level divergence losses such as KL (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). Related distillation methods refine student-data reuse and contrastive objectives (Ko et al., 2024; 2025), interpolate teacher–student distributions (Shing et al., 2025), or use teacher-derived token advantages for preference alignment (Gao et al., 2025). By comparison, RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) often uses sparse binary outcome rewards, which cannot distinguish among failed responses (H¨ ubotter et al., 2026). Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) learns from fixed demonstrations rather than trajectories sampled by the current student. 

Supervision failure on student prefixes. Teacher continuation advantages can diminish as student prefixes lengthen (Li et al., 2026). More broadly, teacher hacking shows that closer imitation of an imperfect teacher on fixed offline data can increase divergence from an oracle (Tiapkin et al., 2025). Complementary distillation methods select difficult positions and adapt temperatures using teacher–student discrepancy (Xie et al., 2026a), gate token-level losses through teacher verification of student proposals (Huang et al., 2026), or adjust forward/reverse KL contributions for individual vocabulary entries (Jung et al., 2025). At the trajectory level, bounded teacher handoffs alter the student’s subsequent reasoning states (Xu et al., 2026). 

Teacher adaptation and privileged supervision. Distillation with privileged context conditions teacher supervision on reference solutions, demonstrations, or environmental feedback (Zhao et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026; H¨ ubotter et al., 2026). CGD trains students to refine flawed responses using teacher critiques available only during training (Kapusuzoglu et al., 2026). Other work jointly optimizes privileged and unprivileged policies with shared parameters (Penaloza et al., 2026). SD-Zero first trains a reward-conditioned reviser through supervised fine-tuning and then distills it on policy, whereas PAST applies teacher RL conditioned on complete failed attempts and reference solutions (He et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026). Prep-OPD optimizes teacher continuations from fixed intermediate student prefixes using suffix-only GRPO, with reference answers used solely to com-pute outcome rewards. The prepared teacher is frozen during student training to provide trajectory guidance and token-level supervision. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented Prep-OPD, which uses reinforcement learning to prepare teachers for continuation from student-generated reasoning prefixes. The prepared teacher guides student trajectories and provides token-level supervision, improving student accuracy across eight mathematical reasoning benchmarks and two student sizes. Our results show that stronger independent problem-solving abil-ity does not necessarily imply better continuation from student prefixes. Controlled comparisons further show that student-prefix preparation improves continuation accuracy and benefits student learning both with and without handoffs. Role comparisons support its value in both trajectory gen-eration and token-level supervision. A.3 EVALUATION AND CHECKPOINT SELECTION 

Student training budget. Both Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-0.6B distillation runs train for one epoch on 16 ,164 problems, with a configured training batch size of 128 . This dataset-level epoch is distinct from the single PPO optimization epoch applied to each collected batch in Table 3. 16 Preprint 

Checkpoint selection. We use a fixed development set of 256 problems for checkpoint selection. These problems are excluded from training. For each student-training configuration, we evaluate eight candidate checkpoints on this same development set and select the checkpoint with the high-est development accuracy. The selected checkpoint is then evaluated on the eight benchmarks in Section 4.1; all eight reported benchmark scores come from that checkpoint. Table 1 and Figures 1(b) and 3(a) use Qwen3-1.7B checkpoints at steps 90 (Standard OPD), 81 (TRD), 117 (IW-OPD and FastOPD), and 36 (Relay-OPD and Prep-OPD). For the Qwen3-0.6B configurations in Table 1, the selected checkpoints are step 110 for GRPO, step 90 for Standard OPD and IW-OPD, step 20 for TRD, step 63 for FastOPD and Relay-OPD( TB ), and step 90 for Prep-OPD. The additional Relay-OPD( T0) comparison in Appendix A.6 uses step 90. For benchmark evaluation, we sample 32 responses per problem on the smaller benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AIME 2026, AMC 2023, HMMT February 2026, and HMMT November 2025), and 4 responses per problem on the larger benchmarks (OlympiadBench and MATH-500). These sampling counts apply to all evaluated student configurations. We report mean sampled-response accuracy for each benchmark and the unweighted mean across the eight benchmarks. 

Implementation checks. Five acceptance tests were run before any training arm, all passing: exact-prefix token identity between construction and continuation (32/32 static and 32/32 clean-continuation assertions); suffix-mask correctness on real pipeline tensors; the reward parser (12 assertions and 11 observational cases covering multi-boxed answers, prefix-wrong-suffix-correct re-lay shapes, unit and format variants, and non-crashing behavior on empty/noisy inputs); teacher isolation (checkpoints contain student-actor weights only); and checkpoint reload parity (all 310 floating-point tensors within 3.8 × 10 −6 of the base, consistent with two AdamW steps at lr 10 −6). A.4 TEACHER CONDITIONAL -CAPABILITY EVALUATION 

Figure 4 and Table 5 report teacher problem-start and student-prefix continuation accuracy across the eight benchmarks in Section 4.1. The two RL teachers share the same initialization, training problem pool, outcome verifier, and update budget; their training condition is problem-start solving for TB and student-prefix continuation for TP . We construct a single evaluation manifest with seed 

42 from fixed S0 outputs. All three teachers, T0, TB , and TP , share the same problem set and prefix collection. They correspond to t0 , tb , and tr , respectively, in the evaluation configuration. The evaluation conditions are problem-start solving (clean, or 0% ) and continuation from prefixes at 20% , 40% , 60% , and 80% of the fixed student response’s token length. These fixed evaluation positions differ from the randomized training-prefix construction in Appendix A.1. Prefix validity checks follow that construction protocol. No teacher-specific triggers or subsequent correctness outcomes select the states. Problem-start solving generates a full response from the problem alone; student-conditioned con-tinuation generates a suffix from the frozen prefix, with the final answer verified over the complete prefix and suffix. The two conditions use the same decoder (temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0) and the same generated-token cap. The prefix includes computation already spent by the student, so the comparison measures conditional capability rather than equal-total-inference-cost accuracy. Both conditions for a given teacher use the same final checkpoint. Continuation samples are first averaged within problem and depth, then over the evaluated problems within each benchmark. Each benchmark’s continuation accuracy is the equal-weight mean across the four depths; overall accuracies weight the eight benchmarks equally. Independent accuracy averages over evaluated problems within each benchmark and then weights the eight benchmarks equally.
