# Rethinking Self-Distillation for Multi-Teacher Capability Merging 

Roy Xie 1,2, Dan Friedman 1, Feng Nan 1, Yukun Huang 1, Zhichao Xu 1, Chengjiu Zhang 1, Jun Xu 1,

Manaal Faruqui 1, Vivek Rathod 1, Bhuwan Dhingra 1,21Apple, 2Duke University Combining capabilities of multiple expert models trained starting from the same base checkpoint has become increas-ingly common in frontier language-model post-training. Recent trends suggest that multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) outperforms conventional off-policy methods. However, despite the higher inference and environment interac-tion costs incurred by MOPD, we find that much of its reported accuracy gain is due to certain training design choices and hyperparameter optimization, rather than the algorithm itself. We conduct a controlled self-distillation study across two multi-teacher settings, four models, and eleven benchmarks, comparing off-policy methods, namely supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and soft-label distillation, to hybrid teacher-prefix distillation and MOPD. We found that all four methods achieve nearly identical accuracy. However, MOPD uses 14 .8–23 .1× SFT’s training GPU-hours. We also revisit four recently published comparisons between on-policy and off-policy distillation and find that the reported on-policy gains shrink substantially when SFT baselines are trained on rejection-sampled teacher trajectories and use independently tuned hyperparameters. As a training-free alternative, we also find that simple weight-merging methods can recover expert capabilities with minutes of CPU merging time, although their accuracy degrades as model size decreases and task interference increases. Overall, our results question recent gains reported due to MOPD and suggest careful tuning of more efficient off-policy baselines as a viable alternative.

## 1 Introduction 

Modern frontier large language model (LLM) post-training increasingly decomposes capability development into domain-specific training (DeepSeek AI, 2026; Kimi Team, 2026; NVIDIA, 2026). Starting from a shared checkpoint, each expert can be optimized through reinforcement learning (RL) with its own algorithms, prompts, rewards, and training infrastructure, making capability hill-climbing modular and parallelizable. Transferring these capabilities back into a student initialized from the same checkpoint is also known as 

self-distillation (The Microsoft AI Team, 2026). However, recent releases report the use of different self-distillation recipes. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) supervises the student on its own generated trajectories (Ma et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026) and is increasingly adopted (Lim et al., 2026; Park et al., 2026; NVIDIA, 2026), including in GLM-5 (GLM-5-Team et al., 2026), DeepSeek-V4 (DeepSeek AI, 2026), and Kimi K3 (Kimi Team, 2026). In contrast, MAI-Thinking-1 (The Microsoft AI Team, 2026) and Step 3.5 Flash (Huang et al., 2026) use off-policy supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on teacher-generated trajectories. The choice of distillation method affects both the compute budget and the infrastructure required for post-training. MOPD requires repeated student generation and teacher scoring throughout training (Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025; Chen et al., 2026). For agentic tasks, each rollout may also require live envi-ronment interaction, with costs that grow with trajectory length and the number of environments. Performing rollouts across multiple environments in the same training run also requires complex training infrastructure.

Off-policy distillation instead reuses fixed expert trajectories, incurring one-time collection costs upfront with no training-time environment interaction. The two approaches differ substantially in both cost and training dynamics. While some work has suggested that OPD outperforms off-policy methods (Ma et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026), there have been limited systematic comparisons under a controlled setup. We therefore ask: 

What is the most effective way to consolidate expert capabilities into a single general-purpose model? 

To assess whether on-policy training is worth its additional cost, we first ask whether its reported gains persist against well-tuned off-policy baselines. Several recent comparisons do not report their SFT tuning procedure (Gao et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c). Inspecting the released SKD code (Xu et al., 2025), we find that it includes prompt tokens in the SFT loss and uses a shared learning rate for SFT and OPD 1. We revisit four published studies to assess how their reported on-policy gains change with stronger SFT recipes. We tune SFT and OPD learning rates independently and train with rejection-sampled expert trajectories (§6.4). Figure 1 shows that these changes substantially reduce the accuracy gaps between SFT and OPD, raising the question of how much of the reported advantage comes from on-policy supervision itself. Therefore, we conduct a comparison under shared initialization through a controlled self-distillation setup for four methods (§3.1): two off-policy approaches, SFT on teacher-generated trajectories and soft knowledge dis-tillation (Soft-KD) using full teacher next-token distributions; hybrid Offline-MOPD, which samples student responses conditioned on teacher-generated prefixes; and on-policy MOPD. Shared initialization also makes weight merging a natural alternative: it directly combines expert parameters without training (Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2023) 2. We study two multi-teacher settings: MIA , which combines mathematics, instruction following, and agentic tasks; and Agent ×3, which combines ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2020), WebShop (Yao et al., 2022), and InterCode (Yang et al., 2023), where every on-policy rollout requires live environment interaction. We evaluate four models from the Qwen and Ministral families (1.7B to 8B) on eleven single-turn and multi-turn benchmarks. We find that, with careful hyperparameter tuning, SFT, Soft-KD, Offline-MOPD, and MOPD achieve comparable accuracy and show similar out-of-domain retention (§5.1). However, MOPD requires 14 .8× and 23 .1× the training GPU-hours of SFT on MIA and Agent ×3, respectively (§5.2). As an efficient alternative, weight merging achieves capability recovery comparable to distillation, although its effectiveness declines at smaller model scales and when merging experts with overlapping capabilities (§5.3). With larger teachers, MOPD recovers a smaller fraction of the teachers’ in-domain gains, while SFT maintains high recovery at the cost of greater out-of-domain degradation (§6.1). Finally, we analyze attention-weight changes and model behavior to characterize differences among the methods beyond accuracy (§6.2). Overall, our results question the reported benefits of MOPD and show that well-tuned off-policy and training-free methods offer efficient alternatives for merging expert capabilities.

22 Related Work  

> Off-Policy and On-Policy Knowledge Distillation.

Off-policy distillation typically trains a student on teacher-generated sequences, using either hard-token targets or teacher probability distributions (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016). Because the student generates its own prefixes during inference, it may encounter mismatch in states from the training data, resulting in accumulating errors (Ross et al., 2011; Bengio et al., 2015). On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this mismatch by providing teacher supervision on student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Song and Zheng, 2026). Intermediate approaches interleave teacher and student tokens (Xu et al., 2025) or combine student sampling with replayed trajectories and prefixes (Ko et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2026). Recent studies also identify teacher–student compatibility (Li et al., 2026b) and data selection (Hou et al., 2026) as important factors in OPD accuracy, and Ding and Zhang (2026) recover most of the OPD gain in an ablation that assigns a fixed negative advantage to selected low-probability student tokens. However, systematic comparisons across these approaches remain limited when multiple teachers and the student share the same initialization. We discuss additional work on SFT and distillation in Appendix B.  

> Multi-Teacher Capability Integration.

MOPD combines specialized capabilities by using each domain expert to supervise student-generated trajectories (Ma et al., 2026; Lim et al., 2026). This approach is also used in large-scale post-training (Park et al., 2026; NVIDIA, 2026). Alternatives include distilling expert trajectories through SFT (The Microsoft AI Team, 2026) and directly combining expert weights without additional training (Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2023; Yadav et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024), which Wang et al. (2026a) weigh against joint multi-domain RL. Closest to our work, MOPD (Ma et al., 2026) and Open-MOPD (Gao et al., 2026) compare on-policy distillation with offline fine-tuning and weight merging for integrating experts trained from a shared initialization. However, these studies do not systematically compare computational costs across methods, and it is unclear to what extent each baseline is independently tuned. For example, using released data and evaluation protocols from Open-MOPD, we find that hyperparameter sweeps and rejection-sampled data can bring SFT to OPD-level accuracy (§6.4). 

## 3 Methodology 

We study multi-teacher self-distillation (Figure 2), which combines the capabilities of K domain experts into a student πθ initialized from a shared base checkpoint π0 = πθ0 . The domain experts πd = πθd share this base initialization and provide supervision for student training. Let pθd(· | x) denote the trajectory distribution induced by policy πθ on task x in domain d, including environment interactions for agentic tasks. A single-turn trajectory τ contains one response to a fixed prompt. A multi-turn trajectory alternates model actions and environment observations, so later decisions depend on feedback from earlier actions and the growing interaction history. Off-policy distillation trains the student on recorded teacher trajectories, avoiding student rollout generation during training but restricting supervision to teacher-visited contexts. On-policy distillation instead queries the teacher at student-generated contexts, which can cover states absent from the recorded trajectories but require additional generation and live environment interaction for agentic tasks. To construct the offline corpus Dd, we sample trajectories from expert πd for domain-d training prompts and retain successful task– trajectory pairs (x, τ ). We collect this rejection-sampled corpus (Yuan et al., 2023) once and hold it fixed across methods: all four use its prompts, SFT and Soft-KD use its recorded trajectories, and Offline-MOPD samples student responses conditioned on its recorded prompts and interaction histories. Teacher trajectory generation costs are discussed in §5.2; we ablate rejection sampling in §6.3.

## 4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Multi-Teacher Setup 

We study two multi-teacher setups: MIA combines mathematics, instruction-following, and agentic experts, mixing disjoint capabilities, while Agent ×3 combines ALFWorld, WebShop, and InterCode-SQL experts (Shridhar et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023) that all focus on long-horizon agentic capability. We study four models under both multi-teacher setups: Qwen3-8B , Qwen3-1.7B (Qwen Team, 2025), 

Qwen3.5-4B-Base (Qwen, 2026), and Ministral-3-3B-Instruct (Mistral AI, 2025); we conduct RL training on each base model to obtain the teachers. The mathematics teacher is trained with DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) on DAPO-Math-17k (ByteDance Seed and Tsinghua AIR, 2025), the instruction-following teacher with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) on the IF-RLVR multi-constraint data (Allen Institute for AI, 2025), and the three agentic teachers with GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) on the training data of their environments. As a multi-task reference, we include Mix-RL, a single model optimized jointly on all domains (Wang et al., 2026a). All four distillation methods use prompts from the same rejection-sampled teacher corpus; Appendix D.1 details its construction. 

4.2 Hyperparameter Tuning 

Our goal is to train each method to its best possible accuracy, so that the comparison is controlled. Each of the four distillation methods is tuned independently and trained for five epochs, as detailed in Appendix D.2. Besides method-specific tuning, such as the reverse-KL coefficient shared by the two MOPD variants, we focus on tuning the learning rate, as the methods differ in gradient scale and a shared rate would handicap some of them. We sweep up to five values per method, model, and setup, giving at most 160 configurations 3

before seed replication or additional sweeps. Its effect is large: the learning rate alone varies the SFT validation score by up to 11.3 percentage points (§6.3). 4.3 Evaluation 

In our self-distillation setup, every task is split into disjoint training, validation, and test sets. We se-lect the checkpoints with the highest validation-set task accuracy and evaluate them on held-out test sets; Appendix D.3 details the selection procedure. We report accuracy on eight single-turn benchmarks: MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), AIME24 and AIME25 (Mathematical Association of America, n.d.), IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2025), GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2024), and MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024); and on three multi-turn bench-marks: InterCode-SQL, ALFWorld, and WebShop, with ALFWorld’s seen and unseen splits. Appendix D.4 provides the split definitions and the benchmark suite with its metrics and test-set sizes. Every benchmark counts as either in-domain or out-of-domain, based on the teachers’ domains. We summarize each model by its recovery rate over all in-domain benchmarks and its retention over all out-of-domain benchmarks, as defined in §3.3. We report three training seeds for the four distillation methods and Mix-RL, and three evaluation seeds for the base, teachers, and merged models.

5.2 Distillation Efficiency 

Ma et al. (2026) report each method’s accuracy against training steps. However, step counts alone do not capture computational cost across distillation methods when each one is individually tuned. For example, learning rates affect convergence speed, batch sizes determine steps per epoch, and on-policy updates also require student generation and teacher scoring. We therefore report cumulative GPU-hours on eight B200 GPUs (Appendix D.2). Figure 3 compares validation accuracy against training progress and cumulative GPU-hours for Qwen3-8B . At the full configured budgets, MOPD and SFT use 383.32 and 25.90 GPU-hours on 

MIA (14 .8×), and 415.80 and 18.00 GPU-hours on Agent ×3 ( 23 .1×), respectively (Table 19). Student rollout accounts for 86.5–96.0% of OPD step time (Table 18). The outlined markers retain the original best validation checkpoints; their costs exclude subsequent training and checkpoint selection. It is worth noting that reported training GPU-hours do not include one-time teacher trajectory generation, as the teacher costs for on-policy and off-policy methods are not directly comparable. SFT’s data-generation cost is incurred once and amortized across multiple training runs and hyperparameter searches. On the other hand, MOPD requires teacher forward passes to score new student trajectories in every training run. 5.3 Alternatives to Distillation 

We compare distillation methods with weight merging and Mix-RL to assess whether expert capabilities can be recovered without distillation training. Weight merging is more effective at the larger model scale: averaged over both settings, Qwen3-8B recovers 99.0% of the teachers’ gains, compared with 87.3% for Qwen3-1.7B 

(Table 2). Merging also performs better in MIA , where the experts specialize in less overlapping domains: mean recovery across the four models is 96.6%, compared with 87.9% on Agent ×3. Constructing merged weights takes minutes of CPU, but selecting a merge still requires GPU validation (Appendix C). Joint multi-domain RL (Mix-RL) also avoids a separate distillation stage by starting a single monolithic RL training run. It recovers most of the teachers’ gains across all eight model–setting pairs, approaching or exceeding teacher-level performance on Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-8B (Table 2). However, changes to one domain’s training recipe may require retuning the joint run, whereas separate experts support independent iteration before integration (Ma et al., 2026). 

## 6 Analysis and Discussion 

6.1 Do Larger Teachers Favor On-Policy Distillation? 

A larger teacher may provide stronger supervision while differing more from the student’s initial behavior. To examine whether using larger teachers favors on-policy distillation, we compare 1.7B and 8B experts with a fixed Qwen3-1.7B student (Table 3). With 1.7B teachers, the four methods achieve similar in-domain recovery: 94.7–98.2% on MIA and 96.3–98.3% on Agent ×34. With 8B teachers, SFT and Soft-KD have higher in-domain recovery than both MOPD variants on MIA and the reported Agent ×3 results. The gap is larger on Agent ×3: SFT reaches 99.3% and Soft-KD 93.0%, whereas Offline-MOPD and MOPD reach 78.9% and 73.3%, respectively. On Agent ×3, however, this recovery advantage comes at a large out-of-domain cost. With 8B instead of 1.7B teachers, out-of-domain degradation grows from 5.4 to 14.8 points for SFT and from 4.1 to 11.7 for Soft-KD, with minimal changes on Offline-MOPD (2.6 to 1.1) and MOPD (2.1 to 3.3). On MIA , SFT and Soft-KD experience larger OOD losses at 9.2 and 8.4 points, compared with 3.0 for Offline-MOPD and 2.3 for MOPD. Table 17 provides the per-benchmark results for the 8B teachers. 

6.2 Parameter Updates and Behavioral Changes 

To assess how much each method alters the base model, we measure the Frobenius norm of the attention-weight update relative to that of the base weights (Figure 4a). On Qwen3-8B , all relative changes are below 

0.7% , with merging yielding the smallest changes on average. Small weight changes coexist with substantial shifts in response length (Figure 4b). Using geometric means of paired response-length ratios on in-domain tasks, distillation shortens responses by 23 –29% on MIA and lengthens them by 22 –67% on Agent ×3. MOPD produces the largest response-length change in both settings. Merging increases response length by 3% and 

23% , respectively. Overall, similar recovery scores can accompany different parameter updates and generation behavior.

6.3 What Does It Take to Make SFT a Strong Baseline? 

The strength of an SFT baseline depends on optimization and training data quality. For Qwen3-8B , we sweep five learning rates, selecting the best validation checkpoint within five epochs at each rate (Figure 4c). Both settings peak at 5 × 10 −6; validation scores span 8.5 percentage points on MIA and 11 .3 on Agent ×3. These differences show why SFT must be tuned independently before a performance gap can be attributed to the distillation objective. We also examine rejection sampling, which retains only correct or successful teacher trajectories. SFT recovery gaps are 6.5 percentage points on MIA and 7.0 on Agent ×3, compared with 3.0 and 3.6 for Soft-KD and 0.5–1.6 for Offline-MOPD and MOPD (Figure 4d). Each method has a slightly larger gain on Agent ×3 than on MIA . The result shows filtering benefits off-policy more than on-policy methods. 

6.4 Do Reported On-Policy Gains Persist with Stronger SFT Baselines? 

We revisit the SFT–OPD comparisons in Open-MOPD (Gao et al., 2026), SKD (Xu et al., 2025), TCOD (Wang et al., 2026c), and ReOPD (Liao et al., 2026) to assess whether reported on-policy gains persist with independently tuned SFT baselines and rejection-sampled teacher trajectories. Only Open-MOPD shares our multi-teacher self-distillation setting, with domain experts trained from the student’s initial checkpoint; the SKD, TCOD, and ReOPD settings we reproduce each distill a single larger teacher into a smaller student. The results show SFT gains of 1.3–6.9 percentage points after tuning (Table 4). Relative to tuned SFT, the RS recipes gain 7.0 points on Open-MOPD, 4.4 on TCOD, and 0.6 on ReOPD, and lose 0.8 on SKD. Open-MOPD, TCOD, and ReOPD reproductions use reporting benchmarks for tuning; our main study uses held-out validation sets. These recipe comparisons also differ in data, training budgets, or evaluation details (Appendix A). 97 Conclusion 

Across four models and two task compositions, we find no consistent in-domain recovery advantage for MOPD over tuned off-policy distillation. For Qwen3-8B , MOPD uses 14 .8× and 23 .1× SFT’s training GPU-hours at the full configured budgets on MIA and Agent ×3, respectively. Revisiting published comparisons also narrows their reported on-policy gains, with residual gaps that depend on the task and baseline recipe. Weight merging offers a training-free alternative, but its performance varies with model size and task composition. Overall, we question recent gains reported due to MOPD and suggest careful tuning of more efficient off-policy and training-free baselines as viable and cheaper alternatives. We discuss limitations in Appendix H.

15 A Reproducing Four Published SFT--OPD Comparisons 

We describe the reproduction setups for the four SFT–OPD comparisons in Figure 1 and Table 4. The subsections report individual benchmark or split results with the procedures used to obtain them. We use released artifacts where available. Our scope is only SFT–OPD comparisons, not an assessment of each study’s proposed method or broader contributions. 

A.1 Open-MOPD  

> Models and data.

Open-MOPD integrates mathematics, code, and instruction-following capabilities by using domain experts to supervise student-generated responses (Gao et al., 2026). We initialize both methods from the released SmolLM3-3B MixSFT checkpoint and reuse the released experts and evaluators. MixSFT is the published SFT reference, distinct from the paper’s rejection-fine-tuning (RFT) baseline. The published MixSFT recipe uses unfiltered R1 and Nemotron traces and roughly balances response tokens across math-ematics, code, and instruction following (37:28:35, approximately 1:1:1). The naive OPD sampler instead allocates prompts approximately 2:2:1; these are different units of mixture balance.  

> Training and evaluation.

Following the released recipe, we train the SFT baselines up to seven epochs. Learning rates and epoch budgets are selected on the reporting benchmarks and then fixed across three RS SFT training runs. We report their seven-epoch checkpoints and three OPD runs at update 200, using the released OPD rate of 1.5 × 10 −6. The original and rejection-sampled SFT recipes differ in both teacher data and training budget. We evaluate on AIME 2024/2025, LiveCodeBench v5/v6, and IFEval/IFBench. A.2 SKD  

> Models and data.

SKD interleaves student-proposed tokens with teacher replacements to construct distillation trajectories (Xu et al., 2025). We reproduce its SFT and OPD baselines on GSM8K. We use a Gemma-2B-it student and a Gemma-7B-it teacher. The release provides training code but no teacher checkpoint, so we retrain the teacher on the authors’ 7,000-example training set. The published SFT baseline uses 1,000 gold solutions, while OPD supervises student responses to the same prompts. For teacher-data SFT, we generate one greedy response per prompt and compare the full corpus with a rejection-sampled corpus that retains responses matching the gold answer. Filtering drops unsolved prompts without resampling, changing both corpus size and prompt coverage.  

> Training and evaluation.

The released SFT loss includes prompt tokens, whereas the distillation trainer super-vises only responses. We train SFT with response-only loss, disable packing, and match the distillation batch 16 size of eight. The release uses a shared learning rate of 10 −5 without a documented SFT-specific sweep; we select learning rates independently and select checkpoints using validation accuracy. In separate matched controls, each method receives 375 updates at global batch size eight. These controls use two teacher draws and three training seeds, with learning rates selected for each method and teacher using a separate sweep seed and the grid {3 × 10 −6, 5 × 10 −6, 10 −5, 2 × 10 −5, 5 × 10 −5}. Checkpoint selection maximizes development accuracy, breaking ties in favor of the earlier step. We evaluate on the 1,319-item GSM8K test set with greedy decoding, a 512-token limit, and a double-newline stop sequence. Baseline reproductions use the released grader, which requires the entire answer line after #### to match the gold answer. Teacher-data comparisons and matched controls instead extract the first number on that line, preventing trailing role markers from invalidating a correct numeric response. 

A.3 TCOD  

> Models and data.

TCOD gradually expands the portion of an interaction trajectory assigned to the stu-dent during on-policy distillation (Wang et al., 2026c). We reproduce its SFT and OPD baselines on ALF-World. We use a Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct student. Both methods use an ALFWorld-trained Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct teacher. We collect teacher trajectories on the authors’ 3,553 training games, allowing up to six attempts per game and retaining 3,342 successful trajectories for SFT. OPD generates student rollouts on the full training game list. The release provides OPD code; we reconstruct the SFT pipeline from the teacher trajectories.  

> Training and evaluation.

Our SFT recipe treats each teacher action as a separate target and supervises actions throughout the episode. This replaces the framework’s default multi-turn formatting, which supervises only the final assistant message. SFT trains for two epochs with a 4,096-token limit, while OPD trains for 250 updates. We tune the learning rate for each method on the reporting splits, selecting 2 × 10 −5 for both; the released OPD rate is 10 −6. The selected rates and training budgets are fixed across subsequent runs, and we report terminal checkpoints. The SFT development split is constructed by turn and can share games with training. Both methods use the released accumulate protocol, which retains the full dialogue and repeats the two latest observation–action pairs in each new prompt.

## B Additional Related Work 

Sequence-level distillation trains students on teacher-generated outputs (Kim and Rush, 2016). Later work enriches supervised training with synthetic instruction–response pairs, as in Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2023), and teacher-generated rationales, as in Distilling Step-by-Step (Hsieh et al., 2023). Selecting the generated examples is also consequential: STaR and rejection-sampling fine-tuning retain reasoning traces that yield correct answers (Zelikman et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023); RAFT selects examples by reward (Dong et al., 2023); and ReST EM alternates generation, filtering with binary feedback, and fine-tuning (Singh et al., 2024). The strength of a supervised baseline also depends on optimization: well-tuned baselines can outperform newer methods (Melis et al., 2018), where the rankings can change with the hyperparameter-search budget (Dodge et al., 2019). In this work, we examine how these data and tuning choices affect reported on-policy gains and whether on-policy supervision improves capability recovery for self-distillation setup.

## D Experimental Details 

D.1 Teachers and Distillation Corpus 

In the main self-distillation experiments, teachers share their student’s initialization, tokenizer, and chat template. Each teacher is RL-trained from that initialization, and we evaluate the selected teacher checkpoints using the same evaluation harness as the students. For each student and setup, we construct one rejection-sampled teacher corpus (Yuan et al., 2023): we sample teacher trajectories and retain at most one trajectory with verified reward 1 per prompt. Generation costs are reported in Appendix G.2. SFT, Soft-KD, and Offline-MOPD use the recorded prompt–trajectory pairs; MOPD uses the same prompts and generates its own trajectories during training. We verify that the prompt sets match by comparing their hashes. All four methods route prompts to teachers by the known task domain, so the comparison measures capability integration independently of router quality. 

D.2 Training 

We train SFT and Soft-KD with TRL (von Werra et al., 2020). Offline-MOPD, MOPD, and RL teacher training use verl (Sheng et al., 2025), with verl-agent for multi-turn tasks (Feng et al., 2025). We use vLLM to serve student rollouts and teacher scoring in the two MOPD variants (Kwon et al., 2023). MOPD uses one rollout per prompt and optimizes per-token reverse KL (Equation (3)). Offline-MOPD uses the same trainer as MOPD but replaces live environment interaction with replay of recorded teacher observations: in Agent ×3, MOPD executes student actions to obtain fresh environment observations, whereas Offline-MOPD conditions each student response on the recorded teacher history. ## E Why Off-Policy Distillation Can Match On-Policy Distillation 

Motivated by these empirical results, we analyze when off-policy distillation can remain competitive despite the distribution mismatch between teacher-generated training trajectories and student rollouts. 

E.1 Exposure Bias and Prior Work 

Teacher-generated trajectories have long been used to distill multiple RL policies into a single student (Rusu et al., 2016). When teacher and student share a base model, SFT can match RL expert performance without a clear advantage from on-policy distillation (Zhang et al., 2026). Agentic studies similarly report successful single-task transfer, but observe degradation when combining tasks (Wang et al., 2026b). Our multi-teacher experiments show that tuned off-policy methods can still recover expert capabilities comparably to MOPD. However, the concern about exposure bias remains: off-policy training uses teacher-generated trajectories, but student actions determine the states encountered during generation. Early errors can shift the later trajectory away from the training distribution. We therefore analyze whether low token loss on teacher trajectories keeps student and teacher rollout distributions close despite exposure bias.

## H Limitations 

Due to limited resources, our main study covers four models from 1.7B to 8B parameters and two multi-teacher settings. More diverse teacher setup beyond MIA and Agent ×3 are not tested. The larger-teacher comparison uses one 1.7B student and 8B teachers from the same model family. These results do not establish whether off-policy distillation remains competitive at larger scales or with less compatible teachers.
