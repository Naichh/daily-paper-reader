ABSTRACT
On-policy distillation (OPD) improves large language model reasoning by train-
ing students on their own rollouts with dense token-wise supervision from the
teacher. However, token-wise OPD does not explicitly provide a coherent alter-
native reasoning step showing how the student’s step could be revised to improve
subsequent reasoning. Furthermore, this paradigm can become less effective when
the student produces a degenerate reasoning prefix, as subsequent teacher super-
vision remains conditioned on that prefix and may reinforce poor reasoning pat-
terns. In this work, we focus on learning reasoning revision with segment-wise
OPD to rework intermediate reasoning steps and better support subsequent rea-
soning. Through controlled reasoning interventions, we find that replacing student
segments with teacher redrafts improves subsequent reasoning accuracy. There-
fore, we address the problem of turning teacher redrafts into explicit supervision
for learning to revise reasoning. We propose Segment-wise On-Policy Distilla-
tion (SEG-OPD), which selects student segments based on an uncertainty met-
ric and obtains corresponding teacher redrafts. SEG-OPD trains the student to
prefer teacher redrafts over their paired student segments while retaining dense
token-wise OPD supervision. Extensive experiments on mathematical reasoning
and competitive programming tasks show that SEG-OPD-trained students achieve
higher revision success rates than baselines. SEG-OPD consistently outperforms
the compared state-of-the-art baselines in reasoning accuracy with an average rel-
ative improvement of 5.22% across diverse models and tasks. Code is available at
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Seg-OPD.

INTRODUCTION
On-Policy Distillation (OPD) has emerged as an effective paradigm for LLM post-training (Agarwal
et al., 2024). OPD has the student generate its own reasoning trajectories and prompts a stronger
teacher to provide feedback. By integrating the dense token-wise supervision of Supervised Fine-
Tuning (SFT) into the on-policy exploration of Reinforcement Learning (RL), OPD mitigates both
the distribution shift introduced by imitating offline demonstrations in SFT (Ouyang et al., 2022)
and the sparse reward signals of outcome-based RL (Shao et al., 2024).
Despite these strengths, OPD suffers from two inherent limitations. First, because vanilla OPD pro-
vides only local corrections through token-wise supervision, the student is never explicitly shown the
coherent reasoning step that spans multiple tokens. In such cases, local corrections do not directly
show the student how to revise the reasoning step as a whole and redirect subsequent reasoning. Sec-
ond, due to LLMs’ autoregressive nature, their subsequent reasoning is conditioned on the prefix.
Therefore, the existing degenerate prefix from the student rollout can be harmful and thus degrade
the teacher’s guidance, leading to suboptimal student learning. Recent studies show that on de-

generate prefixes, teacher feedback can favor unproductive continuations rather than penalize them,
potentially reinforcing patterns such as repetition loops (Luo et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026).
These limitations motivate us to improve vanilla OPD by replacing unproductive reasoning steps and
redirecting the student’s subsequent reasoning. More precisely, given a prefix and a student reason-
ing segment, the teacher should provide a coherent redraft that revises the corresponding segment
while preserving the prefix. Such a redraft retains the on-policy context that exposes the student’s
current unproductive reasoning mode, providing an alternative reasoning transition that can redirect
subsequent reasoning. We use reasoning revision to describe reworking an intermediate reasoning
step to better support subsequent reasoning, and illustrate it in Fig. 1. In Fig. 1(a), the teacher redraft
offers an equal-split strategy instead of the student’s largest-coin-first approach. Fig. 1(b) illustrates
the use of teacher redrafts as explicit supervision for learning reasoning revision.
Though promising, implementing reasoning revision learning faces two challenges. First, teacher
redrafting is costly, so how can we fully utilize the limited budget to select student segments worth
revising and obtain suitable teacher redrafts? Second, how can the learning objective exploit the
correspondence between student segments and teacher redrafts while preserving the dense token-
wise supervision that makes OPD effective? To address these challenges, we conduct a preliminary
test-time analysis in Sec. 3 to inform our method’s design. We reveal that replacing selected student
segments with teacher redrafts improves subsequent reasoning accuracy for both the initial and OPD-
trained student models. Furthermore, we find that student reasoning pivots associated with high
uncertainty provide more useful revision targets than random anchors.
Building upon these insights, we propose a novel Segment-wise On-Policy Distillation method
(SEG-OPD). To better utilize the limited budget, SEG-OPD uses an entropy-based uncertainty met-
ric to guide student segment selection and obtains corresponding teacher redrafts, providing targeted
revision supervision. SEG-OPD employs a revision learning objective that encourages the student
to prefer teacher redrafts over the paired student segments through a segment-wise preference loss,
while retaining dense token-wise OPD supervision. As Fig. 1(c) shows, SEG-OPD outperforms the
strongest baseline by 2.26% in reasoning accuracy with Qwen3-14B on AIME25.

RELATED WORK
2.1
ON-POLICY DISTILLATION
On-policy Distillation (OPD) trains students on their own rollouts using teacher supervision (Agar-
wal et al., 2024). Recently, OPD has been extended to multi-teacher, multi-task post-training (Xiao
et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026a) and on-policy self-distillation (Zhao et al., 2026a;b). Although OPD’s
effectiveness primarily depends on learning the teacher’s reasoning behavior (Li et al., 2026; Luo
et al., 2026), how these patterns transfer remains underexplored (Fu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026;
Cai et al., 2026). By explicitly guiding the student to prefer the teacher’s redraft over its own seg-
ment, SEG-OPD emphasizes this reasoning behavior during training.
2.2
LEARNING FROM REASONING INTERVENTIONS
As LLMs reason autoregressively, early errors can misdirect subsequent steps, motivating inter-
ventions (Lai et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026b).
Prior work trains reward models with step-level
labels (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), compares reasoning steps for preference learn-
ing (Lai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025b), or introduces teacher guidance during
on-policy training (Xu et al., 2026b; Huang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b). Unlike offline step-
pairing (Lai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024) or interleaving teacher generations within a rollout (Xu
et al., 2026b), SEG-OPD pairs student segments with teacher redrafts, training the student to prefer
these redrafts over its own segments while retaining token-wise OPD on the remaining tokens.

3.3
UNDERSTANDING REASONING REVISION IN OPD
Teacher Redrafts Can Redirect Subsequent Reasoning. As LLMs generate responses autore-
gressively, each reasoning token is conditioned on the preceding context. This dependency suggests
that replacing an incorrect intermediate step with a better redraft can redirect subsequent reasoning.
To validate this, we first manually locate the wrong reasoning segment s in the student rollout, and
then assign the same prefix q to a teacher model to generate a redraft r. For comparison, we prompt
the student to continue the subsequent reasoning from (q, s) and (q, r), respectively. As shown in
Fig. 2(a), replacing s with r increases reasoning accuracy for both initial and OPD-trained student
models, indicating that teacher redrafts can provide direct and effective reasoning revisions.
Reasoning Pivots Provide Better Revision Targets. To extend these benefits to on-policy train-
ing, we need an efficient way to select informative segments worth revising without relying on
manual review. Prior work shows that high-entropy tokens indicate potential turning points in rea-
soning (Wang et al., 2025b; Cheng et al., 2026). Therefore, we select these pivots as anchors and
expand them in both directions to period-marked sentence boundaries, forming a student segment
for teacher redrafting. To examine whether reasoning pivots provide better revision targets, we com-
pare reasoning units centered on (i) random anchors and (ii) pivot anchors around the same rollout
positions. Beyond reasoning accuracy, we also report the revision success rate (RSR), defined as the
proportion of student revision attempts in the complete trajectory that correct an earlier error with a
substantively different alternative. For RSR evaluation, we call GPT-5.6 to extract reasoning steps
from rollouts and assess whether the attempts meet this criterion. Appendix A.1 provides more
details. As shown in Fig. 2(b), pivot anchors yield higher reasoning accuracy and higher student
revision success rates than random anchors at matched rollout positions.

METHODOLOGY
Our preliminary analysis shows that teacher redraft can redirect students’ subsequent reasoning, and
reasoning pivots provide better revision targets. These findings leave two open questions for learning
reasoning revision: (i) How to select student segments and construct corresponding teacher redrafts
for learning reasoning revision? (ii) How to derive the supervision signal from the revision unit to
guide the student in learning reasoning revision?
To address these challenges, we propose SEG-OPD, and illustrate its framework in Fig. 3. As Fig. 3
shows, SEG-OPD first selects student segments around reasoning pivots in student rollouts. For
each selected segment, SEG-OPD prompts the teacher to propose an alternative reasoning step un-
der the same prefix, forming a revision unit from the prefix, student segment, and teacher redraft.
Consequently, SEG-OPD trains the student by optimizing a segment-wise preference learning ob-
jective on these revision units while retaining token-wise OPD on the remaining valid rollout tokens.
Next, we present each component in detail.
4.1
REVISION UNIT CONSTRUCTION

using reasoning pivots achieve higher revision success rates and higher reasoning accuracy after
redrafting than those constructed using random anchors at the same rollout positions. Since rollouts
ending in incorrect results are more likely to contain reasoning steps that can benefit from revision,
SEG-OPD restricts candidate segment construction to these rollouts.

complete reasoning segment containing the anchor should exhibit high aggregated uncertainty, rather
than being selected because of an isolated high-entropy token. We first quantify uncertainty at each

Teacher Redrafting. To provide a revision as a local alternative to the selected student segment for
preference learning, SEG-OPD constructs a teacher redraft by revising the corresponding student
reasoning step. We obtain this alternative from one full teacher reasoning, allowing the teacher
to develop its reasoning toward the solution. To achieve this, SEG-OPD first provides the teacher
model with both the prefix q and the student segment s. The teacher is explicitly prompted to rewrite
s starting after q and continue reasoning; SEG-OPD then extracts the leading sentence as the teacher
redraft r using the same boundary rule as for student segments, provided that the full draft passes a
task-specific correctness check. The corresponding teacher redraft r, together with q and s, forms a
revision unit p = (q, s, r). Appendix A.2 provides more details on revision unit construction.

To answer RQ1, we compare SEG-OPD with competitive baselines on mathematical reasoning and
competitive programming. Results in Tab 1 demonstrate that SEG-OPD achieves an average relative
accuracy gain of 5.22% over baselines across all models and datasets. Specifically, we observe that:
• Compared with standard OPD, SEG-OPD improves reasoning accuracy in most settings, with
gains of up to 8.24% for DeepSeek-1.5B on AIME24. This improvement demonstrates the effec-
tiveness of SEG-OPD’s segment-wise preference learning.
• SEG-OPD outperforms E-OPD and G-OPD in most settings. Using student uncertainty, SEG-
OPD selects segments around reasoning pivots. It obtains teacher redrafts that offer alternative
reasoning paths, giving students examples of how to redirect reasoning at potential turning points,
rather than requiring students to infer such changes from token-wise distillation signals alone.
• SEG-OPD also outperforms the closely related baseline OmniOPD in most settings, with gains
of up to 8.83% for DeepSeek-1.5B on AIME24. OmniOPD weights student chunks by their
similarity to teacher continuations, whereas SEG-OPD directly learns to prefer teacher redrafts
over their paired student segments.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we address the limitations of standard OPD, where teacher feedback on degenerate
student prefixes may reinforce problematic reasoning patterns without explicitly providing coherent
alternative reasoning steps. Our preliminary analysis shows that replacing student segments with
teacher redrafts can improve subsequent reasoning accuracy. Building on these findings, we develop
an empirically grounded method for learning reasoning revision in OPD, with a learning objective
that encourages students to prefer teacher redrafts over their paired segments. Extensive experiments
show that our SEG-OPD consistently outperforms the competitive baselines in average reasoning
accuracy across diverse models and tasks, while achieving higher revision success rates and fewer
reasoning loops than baselines. Ultimately, SEG-OPD bridges the gap between teacher guidance on
student segments and students’ capacity for reasoning revision, enabling them to redirect subsequent
reasoning when their current reasoning risks becoming problematic.

A.1
DETAILS OF RSR EVALUATION
In the main text, we propose a metric, revision success rate (RSR), in two places: (i). We first intro-
duce this metric in Sec. 3.3 to compare student revision behavior under random and pivot anchors,
and in (ii) Sec. 5.3 to compare student revision behavior during training with SEG-OPD and OPD.
In this subsection, we present details of RSR evaluation. Manual identification of reasoning steps can
be time-consuming and subjective. To avoid this and apply a common evaluation procedure, we use
GPT-5.6 Sol to identify reasoning steps in complete student rollouts, distinguish revision attempts
from other steps, and assess whether each attempt corrects an earlier error with a substantively
different alternative. This process serves solely in RSR evaluation and differs from SEG-OPD’s
boundary selection, since a reasoning step is not necessarily a multi-token span ended with a period,
and vice versa. Here we provide a prompt template for identifying reasoning steps and assessing
revision success.

reasoning steps in their original order.

Preserve the original wording.

error in earlier reasoning.
For such an attempt, determine whether it

Changes in wording or length alone do not count as successful

[Output]

[Problem]
{problem}
[Student response]
{student response}
Simply put, a revision attempt is a reasoning step that seeks to correct a suspected error in earlier
reasoning, whether it succeeds or not. We compute RSR as the number of successful revision at-
tempts divided by the total number of revision attempts, including both successful and unsuccessful
attempts. Steps that do not attempt a revision are excluded from the denominator.
Additionally, we highlight that RSR evaluation and training segment construction serve different
purposes by:
• RSR evaluation identifies steps by their reasoning content to assess revisions in complete student
rollouts.
• Training segment construction uses construction boundaries around pivot anchors to construct
local segments for teacher redrafting, without using the RSR evaluator to determine these bound-
aries.
The preference for complete sentences in evaluation does not require its step boundaries to coincide
with those of the training segments.

A.5
DETAILS OF COMPARED METHODS
In the main text, we compare SEG-OPD with representative and state-of-the-art OPD methods. For a
fair comparison, we reproduce all baselines in the same verl framework, using the student–teacher
pairs, training data, and evaluation protocol described above. We tune each method’s hyperparame-
ters based on student performance on a validation split held out from the training dataset and select
its best validation configuration. This approach ensures that we evaluate each method under its best-
performing conditions. For instance, G-OPD with Qwen3-4B achieves 61.2% on AIME25 under our
experimental setting, compared with 56.1% best performance reported in the original paper (Yang
et al., 2026). Here we present the methods and their implementations as follows:
• OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026): This foundational OPD method trains the student
on its own generated trajectories using token-level feedback from the teacher. We reproduce the
sampled-token reverse-KL objective, with teacher log-probabilities evaluated at every response
token.
• E-OPD (Jin et al., 2026): E-OPD augments standard OPD with forward-KL supervision at po-
sitions where the teacher has high entropy, encouraging the student to retain multiple plausible
continuations. We adapt the authors’ released implementation to our training framework, preserv-
ing the teacher-entropy gate and the forward-KL term over the teacher’s top-k tokens.
• G-OPD (Yang et al., 2026): G-OPD scales the implicit reward defined by the log-probability
difference between the teacher and a reference model. We reproduce this method with a reward
extrapolation coefficient of 1.25 and use a frozen copy of the initial student as the reference model.
• OmniOPD (Zhou et al., 2026): OmniOPD weights entropy-selected student chunks by their
agreement with teacher continuations, using only text outputs from the teacher. We reproduce
its Bayesian chunk weighting and KL regularization from the paper, using ROUGE-1 F1 (Lin,
2004) to measure agreement and the frozen initial student to regularize unaudited tokens.

In the main text, we propose selecting boundaries from student rollouts to form segments. With
selected reasoning pivots, SEG-OPD extends the boundaries from them in both directions until a
full segment is formed. Primarily, we aim to build segments and redrafts that are both integrated
and representative of the underlying reasoning process of the models, requiring us to preserve the
completeness of the segment while ensuring it is informative. Punctuation and line breaks provide
explicit structural cues in model outputs, allowing simple rules to guide both student segment con-
struction and teacher redraft extraction. To achieve this, we conduct extensive analysis to assess the
effectiveness of various construction rules in the context of our SEG-OPD. Specifically, we exam-
ine four different construction rules. We extend the boundaries from the selected pivots until we
encounter the first (i) periods, (ii) commas, (iii) newlines, and (iv) a fixed length of 2, 048 tokens.
We record the results for each construction rule across diverse models and datasets in Fig. 6. From
the figure, we have the following observations:
• Period rule performs best overall, remaining at or near the top late in training. Period boundaries
preserve sentence-level context for teacher redrafting. This helps retain a complete local inference
and its explanation in the paired texts, making the reasoning contrast between student segments
and teacher redrafts clearer for preference learning.
• Comma rule generally trails the period, with a clear gap on HumanEval+. Comma boundaries
may fragment a reasoning step, separating an operation from its justification and obscuring the
reasoning contrast between the student segment and teacher redraft.
• Newline rule approaches period accuracy on Codeforces but remains weaker on HumanEval+.
Prior work uses double line breaks for step segmentation (Zhang et al., 2025), but line breaks can
also reflect layout rather than reasoning boundaries.

B.2
ADDITIONAL ANALYSIS ON REASONING BEHAVIOR
To extend the analysis in Sec. 5.3, we examine how revision learning changes the student’s use of
reasoning transition markers and whether its RSR gains extend to additional model–dataset settings
and a closely related baseline.
We report word ratio, the proportion of English words in natural-language reasoning that belong
to our predefined set, including wait, however, therefore, check, verify, correct,
error, mistake, and incorrect. Code is excluded from this calculation. Prior work uses
related expressions as lexical indicators of reflection, backtracking, and self-verification (Huang
et al., 2026). Motivated by this practice, we use word ratio as a lexical proxy for potential reasoning
transitions and additionally report RSR, as defined in Sec. 3.3, to assess successful revisions.
In addition to OPD, we include OmniOPD (Zhou et al., 2026), a closely related baseline that also
applies teacher supervision to multi-token chunks of student-generated trajectories. OmniOPD uses
similarity with teacher continuations to weight learning from student chunks, whereas SEG-OPD
directly learns to prefer teacher redrafts over paired student segments. We make the following
observations from Fig. 7:
• SEG-OPD increases the use of transition markers across the displayed settings, with higher word
ratios than both baselines late in training for DeepSeek-1.5B and Qwen3-14B. For DeepSeek-
1.5B, the ratio approaches the initial teacher’s level on both TACO and Codeforces. This reflects
increased use of the selected reasoning-related expressions.
• Late in training, SEG-OPD attains higher RSR than OPD in all four additional settings, covering
both mathematical and competitive programming. Successful revisions therefore account for a
larger proportion of the student’s revision attempts across these settings, extending the behavioral
gains observed in the main text.
• Compared with OmniOPD, SEG-OPD generally reaches higher RSR earlier in training, while the
gap later narrows on Qwen3-4B/AIME24 and Qwen3-8B/Codeforces. On Qwen3-8B/AIME26, a
clear gap remains late in training. These trajectories show earlier RSR gains with SEG-OPD than
with OmniOPD, even when their late-stage RSR becomes similar.

small or negative revision margins, whereas the SFT loss measures how well the student predicts
teacher redrafts. By explicitly favoring teacher redrafts over their paired student segments, SEG-

the student which local reasoning choice to prefer under the same prefix, complementing token-

• (iii) Policy entropy. In both tasks, the SFT variant’s policy entropy rises initially and then declines
sharply, whereas SEG-OPD maintains higher entropy in later steps. Lower entropy corresponds
to more concentrated next-token predictions on the sampled trajectories. The SFT variant also
scores below SEG-OPD on all eight benchmarks in Tab. 2, with accuracy gaps ranging from
3.33% on AIME26 to 21.54% on AIME24. Together with the accuracy differences in Tab. 2,

CASE STUDY OF REASONING CORRECTION
In this subsection, we present two case studies of reasoning correction in student reasoning trajecto-
ries. Specifically, we organize these cases with the following settings:
• We present qualitative comparisons between the initial and SEG-OPD-trained student responses to
the same mathematical reasoning and competitive programming problems, using saved evaluation
rollouts.
• We highlight the same specific reasoning error in each response pair and show how the trained
student subsequently corrects it.
• We report the final mathematical answers and the trained student’s programming test results.
For the mathematical reasoning case, we select AIME26 Problem #4, which reduces a counting
problem to finding distinct factors greater than one. The key distinction is between identifying an
equal-factor decomposition and showing that no admissible distinct-factor decomposition exists. We
focus on how the trained response checks a proposed exclusion rule: a correct algebraic reduction
does not by itself justify excluding an entire class of numbers.
For the competitive programming case, we select TACO Problem #797C, where both responses share
the stack representation. The key decision is when to pop the stack: once a character is appended to
the output, later moves cannot change its position. We focus on how the response tests the proposed
greedy rule against the given example and reconsiders which information from the unread input
should guide that decision.

In this work, we propose SEG-OPD, a segment-wise OPD method that enables students to learn
reasoning revision from explicit teacher redrafting. For an asymptotic complexity analysis, we treat
model sizes, batch size, and the number of optimization passes as fixed and assume dense attention
with KV-cached decoding.
Let L be the total length of each problem prompt and its student rollout in an iteration. Student
generation, teacher scoring, and student optimization in OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024) together cost
O(L(1 + L)) per iteration. OmniOPD (Zhou et al., 2026) samples n teacher continuations of at
most c tokens at each of K selected chunks, giving a conservative total bound of O(L(1 + L) +
Kn(L+c)(1+L+c)), including student optimization and reference-policy regularization. For SEG-
OPD, let D bound the number of new tokens in each full teacher draft before boundary alignment;
generating up to K drafts and learning from the revision pairs gives a total bound of O(L(1 + L) +
K(L+D)(1+L+D)). These bounds include teacher-prefix prefill and reference-policy evaluation;
boundary extraction adds only a linear scan of the generated text. Here, K denotes the per-rollout
chunk or segment budget for the respective method; OmniOPD uses K = 10, n = 10, and c = 50,
whereas SEG-OPD uses K = 1 and redrafts only selected failed rollouts. For fixed sampling

E
DISCUSSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
In this work, we propose SEG-OPD, a segment-wise OPD method that enables students to learn rea-
soning revision from explicit teacher redrafting. We conduct extensive empirical analysis across di-
verse model architectures and downstream tasks, demonstrating our method’s effectiveness in learn-
ing reasoning revision and improving student reasoning performance. As with other post-training
methods, SEG-OPD may be misused to adapt LLMs for malicious purposes. We encourage respon-
sible use and appropriate safeguards to mitigate these risks.
Several promising directions emerge naturally for future research, such as extending SEG-OPD to
multi-teacher settings, including those with black-box teachers. The segment-wise objective relies
on preference supervision rather than teacher logits. This would allow multiple teachers, including
black-box models, to provide teacher redrafts for student segments, while a single white-box teacher
supplies the logits required for token-wise OPD. We believe our methodology can provide insights
into exploring these directions simultaneously.
