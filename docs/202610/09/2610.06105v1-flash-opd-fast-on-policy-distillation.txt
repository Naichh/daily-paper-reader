# Flash-OPD: Fast On-Policy Distillation 

Wei Chen 1,2,3 , Junle Chen 3, Yitong Yang 1, Zhaoyang Xu 1, Jiaxin Lin 1,Yuxuan Liang 2, Xiaofang Zhou 3, Kai Wang 1, Rui Chen 1

Tencent Hy, 2HKUST(GZ), 3HKUST On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense teacher supervision on student-generated trajectories, but generating and evaluating long rollouts incurs substantial training cost. Existing acceleration methods reduce this cost through open-loop rollout schedules or closed-loop horizon adaptation. However, supervision compatibility can vary substantially across trajectories, making a single rollout horizon difficult to match their heterogeneous reliable lengths: an overly short horizon may truncate useful supervision, while an overly long one wastes computation beyond reliable regions. Our key insight is that the trajectory-specific reliability boundary need not be predicted before generation. By viewing reliability as the first-passage of accumulated low teacher–student compatibility events, the boundary is inherently unknown before sampling, yet whether it has been reached can be determined exactly from the observed prefix. Building on this insight, we propose Flash-OPD , which shifts from rollout-horizon control to adaptive trajectory-level boundary verification. Flash-OPD interleaves cached generation with teacher verification and independently stops each trajectory according to its observed compatibility events. To reduce verification overhead, the recent event rate is used only to schedule the next verification point, while the actual stopping decision always relies on the exact cumulative count. This separation prevents estimation errors from causing premature termination while enabling efficient verification during generation. Extensive experiments across diverse datasets and teacher–student settings show that Flash-OPD achieves 2.2×–7.5× speedups over standard OPD while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## 1 Introduction 

On-policy distillation (OPD) has become an important component of frontier language model post-training, enabling both capability transfer and the consolidation of specialized teachers (Yang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026). By providing dense teacher feedback on student-generated prefixes, OPD mitigates the training–inference mismatch of learning from fixed teacher-generated responses (Ross et al., 2011; Kim and Rush, 2016; Gu et al., 2024). Practical token-level formulations make this feedback locally available without accumulating future distillation signals into each update (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). However, local supervision does not automatically yield efficient long-horizon training: generating and evaluating long trajectories remains costly, while teacher guidance may become less useful as student prefixes drift into regions of low teacher–student compatibility (Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Recent work addresses this bottleneck by controlling rollout length through predefined schedules (Zhang et al., 2026b,a) or feedback-driven adaptation (Yang et al., 2026c; Liang et al., 2026). Yet these methods select a shared horizon for each batch, despite substantial variation in supervision compatibility across trajectories. Figure 1 illustrates this granularity mismatch: a shared horizon can extend well beyond the compatibility-based boundaries of some samples, even as it adapts over training. Shortening that horizon uniformly may instead curtail useful supervision for other samples. Sample-level prefix filtering offers finer selection, but produces a binary keep-or-drop decision rather than adapting the continuation length of each retained trajectory (Zhao et al., 2026a). Existing OPD acceleration methods commit to a shared horizon before trajectory sampling, causing premature truncation or redundant generation when usable lengths vary across trajectories; Flash-OPD instead verifies reliability during generation and retires each trajectory at its own boundary, ensuring reliability and efficiency. 

Our key insight is that a trajectory’s stopping boundary cannot be known exactly before sampling, nor does it need to be: it can instead be self-verified during generation. We use teacher–student top-k overlap as a proxy for local compatibility and define the boundary as the first position where the cumulative number of low-compatibility events reaches a prescribed budget. This accumulation tolerates isolated disagreements while adapting the boundary to each realized trajectory. Although its location is unknown in advance, whether the boundary has been crossed can be determined exactly from the observed prefix. Moreover, with model parameters fixed during rollout collection, extending a prefix leaves its existing token-level distillation signals unchanged. This enables interleaved generation and teacher evaluation, turning shared horizon into per-trajectory boundary verification. Building on this insight, we propose Flash-OPD , which adaptively schedules verification and independently stops each trajectory within a reliable rollout horizon. At each verification point, Flash-OPD evaluates newly generated positions and checks the cumulative event count. If the budget remains unexhausted, it uses the recent event rate to schedule the next check; otherwise, it stops. Crucially, the estimated event rate determines 

when to verify , whereas the observed count determines whether to stop . For boundaries reached before EOS or the length cap, adaptive verification cannot trigger termination before the defined boundary. Resuming from cached prefixes ensures that each token is decoded only once, avoiding redundant generation. Through verified stopping at the trajectory level, Flash-OPD reduces rollout computation and substantially improves training efficiency over vanilla OPD while maintaining strong performance. Our contributions are threefold: • We identify a granularity mismatch between batch-level rollout horizons and trajectory-specific supervision compatibility, motivating per-sample stopping for long-horizon OPD acceleration. • We propose Flash-OPD , which interleaves cached generation with adaptive verification to determine each trajectory’s rollout length from its observed compatibility events. • Extensive experimental results across diverse datasets and teacher–student settings demonstrate that 

Flash-OPD achieves strong effectiveness, efficiency, robustness, and scalability. 

## 2 Related Work 

On-Policy Distillation. OPD alleviates distribution mismatch by evaluating a frozen teacher on trajectories sampled from the current student. MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2024) formalized this paradigm under reverse KL, while GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024) unified the on-policy / off-policy spectrum across divergences and demonstrated the benefits of on-policy supervision. Subsequent token-level formulations (Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025) showed that OPD can achieve strong reasoning gains at substantially lower cost than outcome-reward RL, leading to its adoption in production post-training pipelines (Yang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2026) and extensions to multi-teacher and self-distillation settings (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b). This makes rollout horizon a natural target for efficiency optimization. Rollout-Horizon Control. A growing body of work therefore treats rollout length as an explicit design variable. Open-loop methods determine the horizon from the training schedule: POPD progressively expands it, TOPD permanently truncates it to a fixed fraction (Zhang et al., 2026b), and prefix distillation trains on short prefixes with a linear ramp (Zhang et al., 2026a). Closed-loop methods instead use runtime signals: 

Prune-OPD derives a drift signal from token-level top-k overlap to guide dynamic truncation (Yang et al., 2026c), while ADWIN selects the shortest window whose prefix gradient remains aligned with the full-rollout update (Liang et al., 2026). Despite their different criteria, these methods make horizon decisions at the batch level, forcing all sequences to share the same rollout length. We argue that this granularity is mismatched with the inherently sample-dependent reliability of teacher supervision. PG-OPD (Zhao et al., 2026a) moves to the sample level by probing fixed prefixes and retaining only high-scoring samples, but yields a binary keep-or-drop decision. In contrast, we infer a continuous per-sample rollout budget within the existing batch-level horizon, enabling each trajectory to terminate according to its own supervision reliability.

3.2 Reliability and Usable Length 

Locality and persistence allow supervision to accumulate gradually, but this does not mean that it maintains equal utility throughout the entire unfolding process. Prior empirical analysis (Li et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c) links successful OPD to progressive alignment on shared high-probability tokens, showing that optimizing only these tokens recovers nearly all the gains of standard OPD. This finding motivates us to use top-k overlap as a proxy for local teacher–student compatibility. Formally, let KSτ and KTτ denote the top-k sets, and define a low-compatibility event as follow:

## 4 Methodology 

4.1 From Horizon Control to Boundary Verification 

Motivation. Existing OPD acceleration methods reduce training cost by shortening rollouts. However, as discussed above, Property ❶ of reliability length, i.e., trajectory specificity , implies that different trajectories have different reliable lengths. For methods that share a rollout horizon across a batch, whether the horizon is scheduled by training progress or adapted from historical feedback, a single scalar is unlikely to fit these heterogeneous boundaries. These findings collectively reveal the inefficiency of existing methods. 4Discussion. The above observations suggest that the granularity mismatch between shared horizon and per-trajectory boundaries cannot be resolved solely by a more sensitive horizon controller. A natural solution is to transform budget allocation into per-trajectory stopping decisions, allowing each trajectory to terminate independently based on its own compatibility events. However, the difficulty lies in the property ❸ of E: it can only be known after the trajectory is sampled, yet the budget must be set before sampling. Nevertheless, property ❷ offers a promising perspective: E is the first-passage time that depends on prefix counting. Therefore, instead of predicting the E of different trajectories in advance before generation, we can verify whether cτ has reached B during trajectory unfolding, thereby naturally deriving a credible E. Locality and 

Permanence ensure that this does not alter the supervision already computed on the prefix. This motivates us to move the stopping decision inside the generation process, shifting from horizon control to boundary verification. 

4.2 Flash-OPD: Adaptive Boundary Verification 

Building on theses insights, we propose Flash-OPD to robustly accelerate OPD via adaptive boundary verification. Rather than fixing unrolling steps pre-sampling, Flash-OPD dynamically stops each trajectory as it unrolls. Alternating between generation and verification , rollouts extend to verification points where cumulative counts are evaluated to either halt or continue. The exact usable length determines termination, while the estimated event rate sets the next verification point.

Complexity Analysis. Flash-OPD replaces a long generation pass with R shorter ones, so the key questions are how large R can be and how much each round costs. Let R denote the number of verification rounds. Since each advance in Eq. equation 5 is at least kmin and k ≤ Lmax , we have R ≤ 1 + ⌈(Lmax − k0)/k min ⌉. When the local event rate is approximately stable, each round consumes a fraction ρ of the remaining allowance, yielding R = O(log B). The per-step cost consists mainly of decoding and teacher evaluation, and our method do not incur additional overhead beyond these. Importantly, with prefix caching, each token is decoded only once, so the generation cost remains proportional to the actual rollout length. Compared with the full rollout length Lmax , Flash-OPD substantially reduces this cost by terminating generation around the usable length E.

65.1 Experimental Setup  

> Datasets and Baselines.

We compare vanilla OPD (Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025), and representative methods for open-loop and closed-loop control: TOPD (Zhang et al., 2026b), and Prune-OPD (Yang et al., 2026c), all trained on DAPO-Math-17K (Yu et al., 2026). Evaluation covers distribution-aligned math benchmarks (AMC23, AIME24/25, HMMT24/25) and transfer benchmarks (MATH-500, Minerva Math, Olympiad-Bench, GPQA), with GPQA testing beyond mathematics. Details are in Appx. D.1.  

> Models and Protocol.

To assess generality across representative OPD settings, we pair DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B with JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B (same-scale, post-training-enhanced teacher) or DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (larger teacher, similar lineage), and Qwen3-1.7B/4B-Base with Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking), varying student scale and Base-to-post-trained distribution gaps. All methods train for 203 steps under identical optimization settings. We report mean@16 averaged over the final three steps (31,744-token decoding budget) and efficiency metrics, including training time. Baselines use recommended hyperparameters, while we use a fixed configuration unless otherwise specified: η = 0 .7, B = 200 , ρ = 1 .0, and k0 = W = 128 . ## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we investigate the mismatch between shared rollout horizons and trajectory-specific reliability boundaries in on-policy distillation. We propose Flash-OPD , an adaptive boundary verification framework that alternates generation and verification, using exact compatibility-event counts to guide stopping decisions and local event rates to schedule verification. Extensive experiments on multiple teacher-student model pairs demonstrate its effectiveness, efficiency, transferability, scalability, and robustness. 13 A More Related Work 

A.1 Distillation Objectives 

Classical knowledge distillation trains a student to match teacher predictions on a fixed corpus (Hinton et al., 2015; Sanh et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). On-policy distillation instead queries the teacher on student-generated prefixes, reducing the mismatch between training states and those visited by the student (Ross et al., 2011; Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). Recent work improves the supervision at each position through reward extrapolation (Yang et al., 2026a), revised divergence objectives (Jang et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026; Ko et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026), and analyses of teacher–student compatibility (Li et al., 2026, 2025; Kim et al., 2026). Self-distillation extends the source of supervision beyond a fixed external teacher (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026). These directions change the training signal, whereas Flash-OPD controls how far each trajectory is generated while retaining the token-level objective on its prefix. 

A.2 Decoding and Training Efficiency 

Speculative decoding reduces generation cost by verifying blocks of proposed tokens. Its verification determines which proposed tokens to accept. The verification in Flash-OPD instead determines whether a student trajectory should continue receiving distillation supervision; it does not replace generated tokens with teacher samples. Improvements to decoding or other training stages (Wu et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026) address costs complementary to rollout truncation. Methods that train models to reason in fewer tokens at inference time (Yang et al., 2026b; Sang et al., 2026) target the deployed model’s response length rather than the length of training rollouts.

B.1 Locality and Permanence 

Lemma 2 (Locality and Permanence) . Fix the student and teacher parameters during rollout collection. At each position τ , the per-position reward rτ, · depends only on (x, y <τ ). Any two trajectories with the same prefix y<τ therefore receive the same reward at position τ , regardless of their later tokens. Proof. By causality, pτ = πθ(· | x, y <τ ) and qτ = πT (· | x, y <τ ) are determined by the prefix. The student’s top-k candidates and their weights are functions of pτ . Both DKL (pτ ∥qτ ) and its top-k approximation are therefore determined by the same prefix. Extending the trajectory changes neither distribution at position τ

nor the resulting reward.

D.3 Baselines 

• OPD (Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025) performs on-policy distillation by sampling trajectories from the current student policy and using a frozen teacher to provide token-level supervision on the student-visited states. We implement OPD following Eq. equation 2 without adaptive truncation, using the response-length limits specified in Table 4. • TOPD (Zhang et al., 2026b) accelerates OPD by truncating student rollouts to a fixed horizon, thereby 17 reducing the number of generated and supervised tokens. We use a fixed rollout limit of 4096 tokens and keep its evaluation budget identical to that of OPD. • Prune-OPD (Yang et al., 2026c) dynamically adjusts a batch-shared rollout horizon based on feedback from previous training steps, reducing unnecessary computation while allowing the horizon to evolve during training. We use threshold 0.7, wdrop = 0 .01 , wbase = 0 .5, initial and minimum rollout lengths of 1024  

> tokens, and a hit ratio of 0.1.

## F Limitations 

While our results demonstrate that Flash-OPD can substantially accelerate on-policy distillation while preserving competitive performance, several limitations warrant further investigation. 

❶ Compatibility criterion. Our current method uses top-k overlap as a practical proxy for measuring local supervision compatibility between the student and teacher. Accordingly, the stopping guarantee characterizes the boundary induced by this criterion rather than directly guaranteeing downstream task accuracy. We adopt this criterion because it can be evaluated online at intermediate prefixes without requiring completed responses, making it particularly suitable for adaptive rollout control. Nevertheless, supervision compatibility may admit richer definitions beyond top-k agreement. Exploring alternative signals that better capture when teacher supervision remains beneficial, while retaining efficient online evaluation, is an important direction for future work. ❸ Beyond token-level supervision. The current formulation is designed for token-level OPD, where supervision compatibility can be evaluated before a trajectory is completed. Extending the same adaptive stopping principle to sequence-level supervision is less straightforward, since outcome rewards or sequence-level judgments generally depend on the completed response. Consequently, directly applying our stopping rule could remove information required to evaluate the final outcome. Developing analogous notions of intermediate reliability for sequence-level objectives may therefore enable adaptive computation beyond OPD and represents an interesting direction. 21
