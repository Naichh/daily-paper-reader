## ABSTRACT 

On-policy distillation post-trains large language model agents by supplying dense, step-level guidance from a teacher policy when the reinforcement-learning reward is sparse and arrives only once per trajectory. Existing instantiations allocate this guidance by the size of the teacher-student divergence at each step, on the single-turn intuition that a large disagreement marks a mistake worth correcting. Once decisions chain over many turns, that rule misfires, since an early drift enters ev-ery later context both policies condition on, leaving the teacher consistent with the drifted trajectory instead of flagging its cause, while interchangeable steps regis-ter large but outcome-irrelevant divergences. We demonstrate this on an agentic benchmark, where distilling the highest-divergence steps brings no consistent ben-efit over random selection. To this end, we introduce GraphOPD , the first method to bring graph-based structural augmentation into on-policy distillation for agent capabilities. It reads which steps enabled which later ones from the environment’s own record of state changes, immune to the drift that corrupts the teacher-student gap, organizes them into a dependency graph, scores each step by a random-walk stationary distribution over it, and fuses that structural credit with the divergence signal into a trajectory-relative mask concentrating supervision on each rollout’s highest-aptitude steps. Across three model scales and eleven baselines on ALF-World, WebShop, and SearchQA, GraphOPD shows competitive performance throughout, improving over the strongest baseline by up to +5.8 pp . An executed-replay audit further shows that this structural credit score tracks true causal impact far above chance, that both fused signals are independently necessary, and that the same signal transfers to out-of-domain tool-integrated reasoning.The code is at

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

On-policy distillation has become a standard way to post-train large language model agents that pursue goals through extended interaction with an environment (Yao et al., 2023), spanning house-hold task execution, multi-step retrieval, and web purchasing (Shridhar et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2018). Reinforcement learning provides the outcome-level training signal for such agents (Shao et al., 2024), and because that signal is sparse and arrives only once per trajectory, on-policy distillation complements it with dense, step-level supervision from a teacher policy, typically a frozen snapshot of the student matched via KL divergence at every step (Zhao et al., 2026; Song & Zheng, 2026; Li et al., 2026c). This combination substantially improves sample efficiency on agen-tic benchmarks (WANG et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026), yet allocating that step-level supervision across a trajectory remains an open and challenging problem central to the whole recipe. Existing agentic methods answer it by weighting each step by the magnitude of its teacher-student divergence, on the single-turn intuition that a large disagreement marks a mistake worth correct-ing (Zhong et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b), while a parallel

A separate family reweights the RL advantage itself with structure read from the rollout, such as branching or graph-based credit scores, but leaves the distillation loss unweighted (Wang et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2025). Across all of these lines the allocation rule remains the single-turn one, in which the size of the teacher-student gap, alone or combined with rollout structure on the RL side, decides where supervision goes. As shown in Figure 1, we test that rule where decisions chain over many turns and find that it fails ex-actly as the structure of agent trajectories predicts , since distilling the k highest-KL steps brings no consistent benefit over distilling k random steps, the divergence-guided rule trails random selection throughout training while policy entropy collapses rather than grows, and successful and failed steps barely separate by divergence. An early drift enters every later context both policies condition on, leaving the teacher locally consistent with the drifted trajectory precisely at the outcome-deciding steps, and interchangeable steps register large divergences unrelated to the result, so divergence magnitude measures how far the student has drifted rather than whether that drift mattered. Agent trajectories nevertheless record what divergence cannot see, namely which steps enabled which later ones, readable from environment state transitions rather than from either policy’s predictions, and no prior agentic on-policy distillation method has used this record to place the teacher’s supervision. To this end, in this paper we focus on placing the step-level supervision where the teacher’s guidance remains usable along a multi-turn trajectory, and propose GraphOPD , the first method to introduce graph-based structural augmentation into on-policy distillation for improving agent capabilities. For each rollout, GraphOPD reads which earlier state changes a later action consumes into a directed dependency graph, converts its random-walk stationary distribution into a per-step structural credit score, and fuses that score with the divergence signal into a trajectory-relative mask that spends supervision where the teacher’s guidance is usable, leaving the rest to the RL objective. The struc-tural score marks the steps at which much of the trajectory’s accumulated dependency structure is resolved, so it stays informative exactly where the teacher-student gap has been blinded by drift or inflated by interchangeable actions. Since both signals come from the rollout itself, the mech-anism needs no extra model calls and enters training as a drop-in reweighting of the distillation loss. We evaluate GraphOPD on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SearchQA across three model scales against eleven baselines, where it shows competitive performance throughout and improves over the strongest baseline by up to +5.8 pp . Analyses further confirm that both signals are indepen-2dently necessary, that the structural credit score tracks true causal impact far above chance under an executed-replay audit, and that the same signal transfers to out-of-domain tool-integrated reasoning. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: • We study the divergence-magnitude rule on multi-turn agent benchmarks and find that the teacher-student gap measures drift rather than outcome relevance, so distilling its highest-divergence steps brings no consistent benefit over random selection, motivating a drift-immune supervision signal for long-horizon agents. • We are the first to bring graph-based structural augmentation into on-policy distillation for agents, reading a directed graph from environment state transitions to capture which steps the outcome turns on. • We design GraphOPD, which fuses a random-walk structural credit score with the diver-gence signal into a distillation aptitude and converts it into a trajectory-relative mask that trains alongside the reinforcement-learning objective. • Experiments across three benchmarks, three scales, and eleven baselines show gains up to +5.8 pp over the strongest baseline, with the structural credit score validated by executed-replay counterfactuals and out-of-domain transfer. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

On-policy distillation for LLM agents. On-policy distillation augments reinforcement learning with dense token-level supervision from a teacher evaluated on the student’s own rollout states (Song & Zheng, 2026; Li et al., 2026c), and the methods we compare against are either instances of it or bounds on what it can add. Objective-level work stabilizes or generalizes the same recipe via target reformulation, entropy-aware divergences, teacher-supported truncation, or reward extrapola-tion (Jang et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c). Among the on-policy methods, OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) distills the student’s own frozen snapshot over its rollouts and SDAR (Lu et al., 2026) applies the same recipe to multi-turn agent rollouts, RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a) routes the teacher-student gap into the RL objective as advantage shaping or update mag-nitude, and OPID (Yang et al., 2026b) distills on-policy from a teacher augmented with privileged skill context. Skill-GRPO and Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026c) injecting external skill demonstra-tions or conditioning self-distillation on them. Each on-policy baseline allocates supervision by the magnitude of the teacher-student divergence, which is the shared premise our empirical study tests. 

Step-level weighting and structural credit assignment. Beyond our comparison set, on-policy distillation has been extended along three axes, controlling when to distill through temporal cur-ricula (WANG et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026), how strongly to supervise each step from the di-vergence through gating, granularity adaptation, or advantage routing (Zhong et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b), and how reliable the teacher is through debate, rollout-tested guidance, or bounded update magnitudes (Wang et al., 2026d; Chen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Sun et al., 2026). A separate family reweights the RL advantage rather than the distillation loss using structure read from the rollout: branching and graph-based credit scores (Wang et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2025; 2026; Li et al., 2026b; da Costa Cunha et al., 2026), dependency and memory graphs (Wang et al., 2026a; Nussbaum-Hoffer et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b), verifier- or Shapley-based edge credit (Wang et al., 2021), milestone and rubric judgments (Zhang et al., 2026a), and bisimulation or transport distances to a reference plan (Wang et al., 2026a). Beyond agent RL, graphs underlie self-correcting knowledge-graph planning (Chen et al., 2024), multi-modal entity alignment (Chen et al., 2020; 2022), temporal or de-correlated recommendation (Zhang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024), and video social-graph generation (Wu et al., 2021). None of these represent, for the distillation loss, which steps the later ones actually depend on, and that is what GraphOPD reads directly from the trajectory. 

## 3 METHOD 

GraphOPD fuses two per-step signals into a single distillation weight. The first is a structural credit signal that identifies which steps in a trajectory were critical to its outcome, derived from a 3Figure 2: GraphOPD overview. From a single rollout, two per-step signals are computed in par-allel, a structural credit signal from the Footprint-built dependency graph and its random-walk sta-tionary distribution, and a divergence signal from the teacher-student gap normalized within the trajectory. The two are fused into a distillation aptitude A(t), converted into a trajectory-relative mask m(t), and the resulting masked distillation loss is added to the unweighted GRPO objective; every stage reads only the rollout itself and needs no extra model calls. lightweight dependency graph built over the steps’ entity-level relations and scored with a random-walk stationary distribution. The second is the divergence signal used by prior on-policy distilla-tion methods, measuring how far the student’s behavior has drifted from the teacher’s. GraphOPD combines the two into a trajectory-relative per-step weight that spends distillation only where the teacher’s guidance is both trustworthy and useful, leaving the rest to the RL objective. Figure 2 summarizes the full pipeline. 3.1 PROBLEM SETUP 

We consider an agent that interacts with an environment over T steps and produces a trajectory 

τ = ( s0, a 1, o 1, . . . , a T , o T ), where at each step t, against environment state st, the agent emits action at and the environment returns observation ot; the action and observation text are what our Footprint extractor reads. A terminal reward R, issued only once at the end of the episode, encodes task success or failure, and no dense per-step reward is assumed. The student policy πθ is trained under GRPO, augmented by a teacher signal at each step. We take the teacher to be πθold , the student’s snapshot frozen at the start of each rollout batch and already used by GRPO for the importance ratio. Staying within one update of the current policy, it serves as an on-support behavioral anchor rather than an external expert. 3.2 STRUCTURAL CREDIT ASSIGNMENT

What the dependency graph encodes. The edges of G record which earlier state changes a later action actually consumes. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

GraphOPD turns the dependency structure of a trajectory into a training signal that existing on-policy distillation methods do not use, and Table 1 shows this signal translating directly into con-sistent gains. The advantage is most direct on the two benchmarks that require sustained manipu-lation of an environment over many turns, where GraphOPD attains the best average success rate on ALFWorld and the best success rate on WebShop at every model scale, ahead of all eleven baselines, peaking at 91.9% ALFWorld success and 83.6% WebShop success at 7B. The margin over GEPO, the closest prior baseline, which reweights the GRPO advantage with a graph-based credit score rather than adding a distillation loss, is positive at every scale, reaching +5.2 to +7.9 pp on ALFWorld and +3.6 to +5.5 pp on WebShop success rate. GraphOPD also outperforms the skill-augmented baselines that receive privileged demonstrations or validated skill libraries, such as Skill-GRPO and GRPO+OPSD, on both benchmarks at every scale, despite reading its own signal for free from the rollout rather than from external supervision. The same structural signal also car-ries over to SearchQA, a retrieval-and-reasoning setting with a substantially different action space, where GraphOPD leads at 3B and 1.7B with 46.1 and 42.9 average accuracy, ahead of every direct-comparison baseline, and stays competitive at 7B. 4.3 ABLATION STUDY 

We ablate four design choices to isolate each component’s contribution, and add a shuffled-centrality control to test whether the gain comes from the structural credit signal’s content or from the mere act of non-uniform weighting. Table 2 reports results on WebShop, SearchQA, and ALFWorld. Divergence-only, which drops the structural credit signal, costs 3.9 to 10.5 pp, so the structural credit signal carries substantial information about which steps matter. Structural-only, which drops the divergence signal, is also necessary at 2.3 to 6.5 pp, and the two signals therefore act as comple-ments rather than substitutes. Soft-Pruning, which replaces the entity-specificity dependency-edge weighting with a constant weight, costs 0.8 to 5.5 pp, less than dropping either signal outright at every one of the nine benchmark-scale cells. Shuffled Centrality keeps the mask’s sparsity and weight distribution but randomizes which step receives which weight. At 7B, and on WebShop and ALFWorld at 3B, it costs more than Divergence-only by up to 1.6 pp, so misassigning the structural credit scores to the wrong steps hurts more than having no structural signal at all. At 1.7B, and on SearchQA at 3B, the pattern reverses and Shuffled costs no more than Divergence-only, most sharply on SearchQA at 1.7B, where the two cost 2.8 pp and 10.5 pp respectively. At the smallest scale, then, the redistribution of supervision weight itself carries part of the structural credit signal’s benefit. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented GraphOPD , which reads a per-step structural credit score from the trajectory and fuses it with the divergence signal to decide where a teacher’s guidance is usable, distilling only each rollout’s above-average-aptitude steps. Across three model scales, eleven baselines, and three agentic benchmarks spanning embodied control, web navigation, and retrieval-augmented reason-9ing, GraphOPD attains the best result on ALFWorld and WebShop at every scale, with a margin over the strongest baseline that reaches +5.8 pp on ALFWorld and +5.5 pp on WebShop, and the same structural signal proves effective on SearchQA as well, extending the approach to a substan-tially different action space. Ablations confirm that both signals are independently necessary, with the structural credit signal the larger contributor. Soft mask. ## C EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND MOTIVATING STUDY DETAILS 

Benchmarks. This paragraph gives each of the three agentic benchmarks’ task format, depen-dency structure, and evaluation protocol. ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) embeds a text inter-face around simulated household scenes, requiring an agent to retrieve, heat, clean, or relocate objects through a fixed vocabulary of navigation and manipulation actions (Table 5). Almost ev-ery action changes which objects and locations are reachable next, so a single early misstep can make the goal unreachable no matter how the remainder of the trajectory is executed, and this is where the dependency-graph hypothesis makes its strongest prediction. We score it by task success rate on the standard 3,553/140 train/test split, with episodes capped at 50 turns. Web-Shop (Yao et al., 2022) simulates an e-commerce search-and-purchase task over a large product catalog, with an action space of free-form search queries and page-element clicks rather than ALF-World’s fixed verb-object grammar. Its dependencies are informational rather than physical, since later clicks depend on which attributes and product identifiers an earlier search surfaced, so the Footprint extractor tracks query tokens and selected products instead of object locations. We re-port both completion score and binary success rate on the standard 1,012/500 split, capped at 15 turns. SearchQA aggregates seven open-domain question-answering datasets, categorized into single-hop factoid questions, NarrativeQA (Kocisk´ y et al., 2018), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), and PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023), and multi-hop questions, HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMul-tihopQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023), formatted as a <search> /<think> /<answer> loop capped at 10 turns. Here dependencies run only through retrieved evidence, with no physical state change at all, which tests whether the mech-anism generalizes beyond the setting that motivated it, and we score it by accuracy over the seven datasets. ## E SIGNAL VALIDATION AND CASE STUDY 

Signal redundancy diagnostic. The fusion in Section 3.4 is a weighted sum of ℓmodel and ℓgraph , so it implicitly assumes the two sources are not strongly redundant , since if they carried near-identical evidence, weighting both by α and 1 − α would double-count the same signal rather than combining two complementary ones. Rather than a separate correlation diagnostic, the ablation (Table 2, Sec-tion 4.3) already tests this functionally, because if ℓmodel and ℓgraph carried near-identical evidence, dropping either one alone, Divergence-only or Structural-only, would cost little, as the remaining signal would recover most of the same information. Instead, both cost a substantial fraction of GraphOPD’s full gain at every scale, Divergence-only 3.9 to 10.5 pp and Structural-only 2.3 to 6.5 pp, and neither recovers what the other drops, so the two signals are complementary rather than redundant. 

Credit-content validation. The Analysis subsection of Section 4 reports the credit-content audit, and this appendix fixes its protocol. 

Counterfactual audit. We construct causal ground truth on ALFWorld by executed replay, re-sampling the policy’s own alternative action at each decision point of a sampled set of trajecto-ries, rolling the environment forward from the resulting state, and recording whether the outcome changes, so a step’s ground-truth contribution is the outcome change induced by its alternative, the same executed-replay principle used elsewhere to audit step-level credit signals (Zhang, 2026). We report, for each of ℓgraph , ℓmodel , and a random score, the Spearman rank correlation ρ with this con-tribution and Hit@10%, the fraction of the signal’s top-10% steps that are truly top-10% impactful, 24 for which chance is 0.10 . The decisive comparison is ℓgraph against the random score, since the claim that the structural credit signal identifies outcome-relevant steps requires both to sit clearly above chance, and the comparison against ℓmodel measures how much the structural credit signal adds over the divergence signal alone. ℓgraph attains ρ = 0 .58 and Hit@10% = 0 .48 , well above the random score at ρ = −0.06 and Hit@10% = 0 .13 and ℓmodel at ρ = 0 .34 and Hit@10% = 0 .41 .

Shuffled-centrality control. To separate credit content from supervision redistribution, the abla-tion (Table 2) includes a Shuffled Centrality row that replaces ℓgraph with a within-trajectory permu-tation of itself, matched to the real one in mask sparsity and weight distribution, and runs the full training pipeline under otherwise identical conditions. Where the real graph retains its advantage over the shuffled one at matched dose, that gain is attributable to the content of the dependency structure rather than to the act of non-uniform weighting itself, and where it does not, part of the gain is attributable to redistribution alone. Table 2’s discussion in the main text reports which of the two holds at each scale and benchmark.
