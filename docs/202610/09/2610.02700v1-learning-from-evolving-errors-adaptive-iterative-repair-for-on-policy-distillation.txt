# ABSTRACT 

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) supplies dense token-level feedback on trajec-tories sampled from the student’s own policy, a richer training signal than the outcome-level rewards of reinforcement learning. This feedback comes from a teacher conditioned on a full reference solution unavailable to the student. The reference solution specifies the target but not how to move from the student’s current error toward it, creating a solution-conditioned shortcut risk . We introduce AIR -OPD, an adaptive iterative repair framework for on-policy distillation that provides error-to-repair supervision. Given a failed response, a guidance generator synthesizes repair guidance for the current error. The student samples an on-policy retry with this guidance. If the retry remains incorrect, the generator produces new repair guidance for the newly observed error. At each round, a fixed teacher receives the guidance as privileged context and supervises the student on an error-aligned region of its latest failed response. Outcome-aware stage weighting favors early repair stages and credits stages whose immediate retry passes verification. We train A IR -OPD on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset and evaluate on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25, alongside out-of-distribution tests on MMLU-Pro and GPQA. We examine two guidance sources, self-guidance from the current stu-dent policy and external guidance from a larger model. For both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, A IR -OPD attains the best mathematical-reasoning averages, improv-ing over the strongest baseline by up to 3.6 points, while preserving base-model performance on the out-of-distribution benchmarks. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is now a standard approach to improving language-model reasoning in post-training (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). However, typical RLVR algorithms such as GRPO (Guo et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024) rely on outcome rewards to compute response-level advantages for policy optimization. Such response-level supervision does not distinguish fine-grained token-level contributions within a trajectory. On-policy distillation (OPD) complements this outcome signal with dense token-level supervision on the student’s own trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) further removes the need for a separate teacher model (Zhao et al., 2026). Through in-context learning, the model serves as its own teacher, conditioned on a reference solution as privileged context that the student never observes. Learning with privileged information uses training-only signals to guide the learner (Vapnik & Vashist, 2009; Lopez-Paz et al., 2016). In reference-conditioned OPSD, a successful attempt already satisfies the verifier, and further conditioning on a static reference may steer the student toward the reference’s solution path. Privileged supervision is thus most informative on failed attempts, and recent work treats successful and failed trajectories asymmetrically (Hou et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). For failed trajectories, a reference solution identifies the correct destination but does not specify the transition from the student’s current error to a valid continuation. When the teacher remains conditioned on that same full solution, the student may learn to imitate privileged 1arXiv:2610.02700v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 Preprint demonstrations rather than acquire generalizable error-correction mechanisms. We refer to this as the 

solution-conditioned shortcut risk .This leads to our central research question: 

How can privileged supervision turn a failed on-policy trajectory into a sequence of learnable error-to-repair transitions? 

To answer this question, we introduce A IR -OPD. Inspired by instructional scaffolding (Wood et al., 1976), A IR -OPD performs multi-round adaptive repair that replaces a static reference-conditioned target with repair guidance synthesized for the student’s latest error. Given a failed response, a privileged guidance generator uses the reference solution to synthesize repair guidance as scaffolding for the identified error, and the student samples an on-policy retry with this guidance. If the retry remains incorrect, the generator synthesizes fresh repair guidance targeting the newly observed error. At every round, the guidance serves as teacher-only privileged context for supervising the student on its latest failed response. A IR -OPD supervises only an error-aligned repair region of that response and excludes tokens that the repair guidance does not target, which would otherwise add noise. Each round thus contributes one stage loss. To combine these losses across rounds, we design outcome-aware stage weighting, which favors earlier repair stages and rewards stages whose retry passes verification. We train on mathematical problems from DAPO-Math-17K (Yu et al., 2025), pairing each problem with a reference solution generated by GPT-5.5, and evaluate on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25. We additionally test transfer beyond this mathematical training distribution on MMLU-Pro and GPQA, and compare two guidance sources, self-guidance from the current student policy and external guidance from a larger model. Across these settings, A IR -OPD attains the best mathematical-reasoning averages on both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B while preserving base-model performance on the out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks. Our contributions are: 1. We introduce A IR -OPD, an adaptive iterative repair framework that aligns privileged on-policy supervision with the student’s evolving errors. A IR -OPD synthesizes error-specific repair guidance and updates it in response to subsequent failed on-policy attempts. 2. We develop an error-aligned distillation objective that uses newly synthesized guidance as privileged teacher context when scoring the current failed response. Each supervision region covers the span where the teacher still expresses correction, and outcome-aware stage weighting allocates supervision across repair stages. 3. Across Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B with self- and external guidance, A IR -OPD improves average mathematical-reasoning accuracy by up to 3.6 percentage points over the strongest evaluated baseline, while keeping average OOD performance near that of the base model. Ablations support the roles of error-aligned supervision and stage weighting. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Building on reinforcement learning from human feedback (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022), reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves language-model reasoning using rewards from automatically checkable outcomes (Lambert et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). DeepSeekMath introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for mathematical reasoning (Shao et al., 2024), while DAPO advances large-scale reasoning training through improved sampling and policy optimization (Yu et al., 2025). Process-level rewards densify this outcome signal by scoring intermediate steps (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a), yet the supervision remains a scalar reward rather than a token-level target. For group-relative methods such as GRPO, difficult samples on which all rollouts are incorrect provide no relative advantage signal for updating the policy. To address this limitation, hint-based RL uses auxiliary guidance to make hard problems more tractable and recover useful training signals (Cao et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2026). StepHint guides exploration with partial solution steps (Zhang et al., 2026a). NuRL injects abstract hints when independent attempts fail (Chen et al., 2026), while Scaf-GRPO provides progressively more detailed hints from a pre-generated, fixed hint hierarchy 2Preprint (Zhang et al., 2026b). HiLL further learns hints conditioned on the reasoner’s current failures, seeking to restore informative reward variation while encouraging transfer to no-hint reasoning (Xia et al., 2026). While these approaches use hints to reduce effective task difficulty and recover outcome-based 

reward signals (from fixed pre-generated hierarchies or from a learned hinter), A IR -OPD synthesizes repair guidance for each failed response during multi-turn repair and uses it as privileged teacher context for token-level supervision of the repair. 

On-policy (self-) distillation. Knowledge distillation trains a student to match a teacher’s output distribution (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016). On-policy distillation (OPD) instead has the teacher score responses sampled from the student’s own policy (Ross et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025), avoiding the mismatch between fixed demonstrations and the states the student reaches at deployment. OPSD extends OPD through in-context self-distillation, where the same model produces teacher and student distributions under asymmetric contexts (Zhao et al., 2026). In the reference-conditioned setting, the teacher receives a reference solution that is unavailable to the student, yielding dense token-level feedback on states that the student actually visits. Subsequent methods adjust the strength or routing of this signal using outcomes, reliability, or trajectory structure (Hou et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c). Closer to our setting, several distillation methods anchor supervision at failures (Xu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). HINT-SD distills a feedback-conditioned teacher on failure-relevant spans selected in hindsight (Yeo et al., 2026), and TAPO inserts a diagnosis-and-correction transition after the learner’s first critical error (Huang et al., 2026). Across these methods, the corrected trajectory or privileged context is constructed in a single pass and does not respond to how the learner fails again. We focus on how privileged context should be updated from one failed retry to the next. 

Self-correction and iterative refinement. Inference-time methods treat an initial response as an intermediate state and use subsequent feedback to revise it. Self-Refine alternates self-generated feedback and refinement (Madaan et al., 2023), while Reflexion converts task feedback into verbal reflections that guide later trials (Shinn et al., 2023). Their effectiveness depends on the information in the feedback, as language models struggle to identify and repair complex reasoning mistakes without external reference signals (Huang et al., 2024). Training-time methods instead seek to internalize improvement behavior. Self-training bootstraps from the model’s own verified rationales (Zelikman et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024; Hosseini et al., 2024), RISE fine-tunes on multi-turn revision trajectories (Qu et al., 2024), SCoRe learns multi-turn self-correction under the model’s own distribution via reinforcement learning (Kumar et al., 2025), and SuperCorrect distills teacher-generated thought templates and correction traces (Yang et al., 2025). A IR -OPD uses iterative repair to construct error-aligned distillation targets on the student’s own failed responses: at each stage, a teacher conditioned on newly synthesized repair guidance supervises the current response.

# 4 AIR -OPD: A DAPTIVE ITERATIVE REPAIR FOR ON-P OLICY DISTILLATION 

AIR -OPD makes explicit the extraction step that OPSD leaves to the teacher (Figure 1). A guidance generator reads the reference solution and writes repair guidance for the student’s current error, and the teacher receives this guidance in place of the full reference solution. The rest of the method answers three questions: how the guidance is updated across repair rounds (Section 4.1), which tokens of each failed response the teacher supervises (Section 4.2), and how the resulting stage losses are combined (Section 4.3). 4.1 MULTI -ROUND ADAPTIVE REPAIR

Training samples contain different numbers of repair stages, and directly summing stage losses would grant samples with more repair rounds a larger total weight. To avoid this, we introduce 

outcome-aware stage weighting . The scheme allocates the stage weights within each supervised trajectory by stage depth and the outcome of the retry immediately following each stage. This allocation rests on two assumptions about the repair process. First, imperfect retries accumulate noise across stages. The later a stage is, the more failed responses and repair guidance accumulate in 5Preprint its context and the less reliable its supervision signal becomes, so the weights should lean toward earlier stages. Second, whether the immediate retry passes verification reflects the stage’s supervision quality. A successful retry indicates that the stage’s repair guidance pointed to a valid repair, and such a stage should receive an additional weight bonus. # 5 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

5.1 MODELS , TRAINING DATA , AND BASELINES 

We use two Qwen variants of different scales, Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025). We train on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset (Yu et al., 2025), generating the reference solution for each problem with GPT-5.5 and retaining the 15,650 problems whose generated solution verifies against the dataset answer. Outcome verification at every repair round uses rule-based answer matching. We compare self-guidance generated by the current student policy with external guidance 

generated by a larger model (Qwen3.5-27B). The main baselines are the corresponding base model and three trained methods. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) trains on the reference solutions with a cross-entropy loss. GRPO optimizes rule-based outcome rewards over groups of on-policy rollouts. OPSD distills a reference-conditioned teacher on the student’s own rollouts. All trained baselines are our reproductions on the same training corpus and backbones (Appendix C). 5.2 EVALUATION BENCHMARKS AND METRICS 

We evaluate models along two dimensions, mathematical reasoning and out-of-distribution general-ization. Mathematical reasoning is evaluated on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 (HMMT, 2025). Each mathematical benchmark contains 30 problems. We report per-benchmark accuracy as Avg@12, with Math Avg. the unweighted mean of the three benchmark scores. Out-of-distribution evaluation uses MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024b) and GPQA (Rein et al., 2024) to assess transfer beyond the mathematical training distribution, reporting accuracy as Avg@4, with OOD Avg. the mean of the two benchmarks. During evaluation, no repair guidance is added to the model input. 5.3 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

We adopt the OPSD training recipe (Zhao et al., 2026), using the frozen initial backbone with its adapters disabled as the distillation teacher. During training, the student operates in non-thinking mode and the teacher in thinking mode. Optimization uses rank-64 LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −6 and an effective batch size of 32. AIR -OPD is built on TRL (von Werra et al., 2020) and trains for 100 optimizer steps. # 6 MAIN RESULTS 

AIR -OPD achieves the best mathematical reasoning at both scales. Table 1 compares A IR -OPD against the base model, SFT, GRPO, and OPSD. Both guidance settings surpass every baseline on Math Avg., and external guidance, where a stronger generator supplies higher-quality repair guidance, exceeds the strongest baseline, OPSD, by 3.6 and 2.7 points at 4B and 8B, respectively. The improvement is consistent across benchmarks. A IR -OPD variants attain the best score on every mathematical benchmark at both scales, with pronounced gains on HMMT25. 

Self-guidance alone accounts for most of the improvement. Without introducing any external model, the self-guided variant already improves over OPSD by 2.8 and 2.1 Math Avg. points. External guidance further improves the Math Avg. The additional gain from external guidance is smaller at 8B than at 4B, possibly because the larger student generates better repair guidance itself. 

AIR -OPD attains stronger mathematical reasoning while preserving near-base OOD perfor-mance. SFT lowers the OOD average by 9.6 and 9.8 points at 4B and 8B, consistent with forgetting under task-specific fine-tuning (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Kotha et al., 2024), and OPSD also regresses at both scales. In contrast, A IR -OPD stays within 0.4 points of the base model at 4B and within 0.1 points at 8B. It is the only evaluated reference-based training method that maintains a near-base OOD average at both scales. GRPO preserves OOD performance comparably, but its Math Avg. trails A IR -OPD by 2.9–4.4 points. One possible explanation lies in the granularity of supervision. RLVR provides only scalar feedback, and full-response distillation supervises every token, whereas AIR -OPD provides corrective signals only on regions aligned with the student’s errors. This selective supervision may limit over-specialization to the mathematical training distribution while preserving the gains of distillation. 7Preprint

# 8 CONCLUSION 

We introduced A IR -OPD, an adaptive iterative repair framework for on-policy distillation that addresses the solution-conditioned shortcut risk of reference-privileged supervision. Instead of conditioning the teacher on a static reference solution, a guidance generator synthesizes answer-free repair guidance that evolves with the student’s errors, the teacher distills each repair over an error-aligned region of the latest failed response, and outcome-aware stage weighting allocates supervision across rounds. Across two model scales, both guidance sources surpass strong RL and distillation baselines on mathematical reasoning while preserving base-model performance out of distribution, and self-guidance alone accounts for most of the improvement. Our analysis further shows that the gains emerge from the first checkpoints of training, and that a small repair budget captures most of the gains observed in our sweep while supervising roughly a third as many samples as OPSD. 9Preprint
