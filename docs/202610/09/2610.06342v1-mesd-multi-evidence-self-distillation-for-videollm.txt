While reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards provides reliable outcome supervision for VideoLLMs, sequence-level rewards offer limited token-level guidance. On-policy self-distillation addresses this limitation by conditioning a self-teacher on privileged information to provide dense token-level supervision. However, aggregating heterogeneous evidence within a single teacher context obscures cross-evidence agreement and conflict. A further challenge lies in determining whether teacher guidance should refine reward-based updates or provide corrective supervision for failed trajectories. To address these issues, we propose MeSD, a multi-evidence self-distillation framework for VideoLLMs. MeSD constructs three evidence-conditioned teachers with shared parameters, using the ground-truth answer as a common semantic context while separately incorporating temporal and spatial evidence. Given the same student-generated prefixes, MeSD evaluates evidence-specific preferences relative to the Answer Teacher and fuses teacher-common preferences with gated teacher-specific residuals. Furthermore, MeSD introduces Verification-Guided Optimization to classify trajectories as Success, Failure, or Indeterminate. For Success and Indeterminate trajectories, MeSD refines token-level advantage magnitudes while preserving reward-derived signs. For verified failure trajectories that contain the required evidence, MeSD applies failure-conditioned distillation, using reverse-KL correction toward the fused distribution. Experiments on multiple video benchmarks demonstrate consistent gains over reinforcement learning and self-distillation baselines.

# 1 Introduction 

Video Large Language Models (VideoLLMs) have made significant progress in video question answering and temporal understanding (Bai et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025a; Ren et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b). However, robust reasoning over complex videos remains challenging (Meng et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026). Such reasoning requires models to temporally identify key events, spatially localize relevant objects, and integrate cross-frame observations to derive the final answer (Meng et al., 2025). Final-answer rewards cannot reliably indicate whether intermediate steps correctly use temporal and spatial evidence, making token-level supervision critical (Lin et al., 2026). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) provides reliable outcome supervision (Guo et al., 2025a), but sequence-level rewards offer limited token-level guidance (Guo et al., 2025b). On-policy distillation (OPD) (Ma et al., 2026) provides token-level supervision on student-generated trajectories, but typically depends on a stronger teacher. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026) avoids this dependence by using the student model conditioned on privileged information as its teacher. However, matching the privileged teacher’s distribution may lead to privileged information leakage and training instability. RLSD (Yang et al., 2026) therefore uses rewards to determine the signs of token-level advantages and teacher signals to refine their magnitudes. However, applying self-distillation to video reasoning exposes two challenges. First, heterogeneous privileged evidence can induce conflicting token preferences along the same reasoning trajectory, as illustrated in Figure 1(a). Combining these sources within a single teacher context obscures their individual contributions, as well as any agreement or conflict between them. Second, existing methods like RLSD use teacher signals to adjust token-level advantage magnitudes while preserving reward-derived signs across both successful and failed trajectories. As shown in Figure 1(b), this merely reweights updates to sampled tokens without directly aligning the student distribution with the teacher distribution, thereby offering limited corrective guidance for failed trajectories. These challenges raise two questions: how to model heterogeneous privileged evidence and when teacher guidance should refine reward-based updates or correct failed trajectories.

To address these limitations, we propose MeSD , a multi-evidence self-distillation framework for VideoLLMs. MeSD integrates evidence-specific preferences and guides optimization based on trajectory verification outcomes and evidence availability. It comprises two components: Multi-Evidence Self-Teacher and Verification-Guided Optimization. The Multi-Evidence Self-Teacher employs three evidence-conditioned teachers with shared parameters: the Answer, Answer-Temporal, and Answer-Spatial Teachers. The ground-truth answer provides a common semantic context, while temporal and spatial evidence are introduced separately. Using the Answer Teacher distribution as a semantic anchor, MeSD captures the token preference shifts induced by additional temporal or spatial evidence. It then combines teacher-common preferences with gated teacher-specific residuals to yield a fused teacher distribution. The fusion selectively retains teacher-specific support while reducing residual contributions when evidence-specific preferences conflict. Verification-Guided Optimization uses a task-specific verifier to classify trajectories as Success, Failure, or Indeterminate. For Success and Indeterminate trajectories, MeSD retains token-level advantage refinement, adjusting advantage magnitudes while preserving their reward-derived signs. MeSD applies failure-conditioned distillation only to verified failures with the required temporal or spatial evidence, using a reverse-KL objective targeting the fused teacher distribution. This objective uses teacher preferences over candidate tokens to provide corrective guidance beyond sampled-token reweighting. Overall, MeSD improves performance across video benchmarks and achieves sustained reward gains during training, as shown in Figure 1(c,d). Our main contributions are: • We introduce MeSD, to our knowledge the first multi-teacher on-policy self-distillation framework for VideoLLMs. Its Multi-Evidence Self-Teacher uses the Answer Teacher as a reference to fuse teacher-common preferences with gated teacher-specific residuals. • We propose Verification-Guided Optimization, which refines token-level advantage magnitudes while preserving reward-derived signs for Success and Indeterminate trajectories. It applies failure-conditioned distillation only to verified failures with the required evidence, using a reverse-KL objective targeting the fused teacher distribution. • Extensive experiments across multiple video reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that MeSD consistently outperforms RLVR, OPSD, RLSD, and video post-training methods.

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Post-Training for VideoLLMs 

RLVR improves video understanding and reasoning through verifiable outcome feedback (Guo et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025a). Video-R1 Feng et al. (2025) and Time-R1 Wang et al. (2025b) target temporal reasoning and grounding, respectively. GKD Agarwal et al. (2024) and Video-OPD Li et al. (2026) supervise student-generated trajectories using teacher distributions to mitigate training–inference mismatch. OPSD Zhao et al. (2026) and SDPO Hübotter et al. (2026) condition self-teachers on privileged solutions and environment feedback, respectively. Visual variants use regional crops (Vision-OPD Yuan et al. (2026)), annotated evidence frames (Video-OPSD Wang et al. (2026)), or denser temporal sampling (S 3T Venkatraman et al. (2026)). Open-o3 Video Meng et al. (2025) jointly supervises answers and spatiotemporal evidence through reasoning traces containing timestamps, objects, and bounding boxes. Its rewards jointly evaluate answer correctness and spatiotemporal grounding quality. Following this paradigm, MeSD provides token-level guidance for student-generated trajectories. 

2.2 Multi-Teacher Distillation and Optimization 

MOPD Ma et al. (2026) combines independently trained specialists, while MT-SDPO He et al. (2026) conditions an EMA self-teacher on verified teacher feedback and AVSD Nguyen et al. (2026) aggregates privileged views. For optimization, VG-OPD Xu and Zhang (2026) uses verification to select eligible experts and localize token-level supervision. RLSD Yang et al. (2026) and VISD Lin et al. (2026) refine token-level credit while preserving reward-derived advantage signs, whereas H 2SD Cai et al. (2026) uses outcome-dependent asymmetric supervision. MeSD measures temporal and spatial preference shifts relative to an answer-conditioned anchor, then fuses teacher-common preferences with gated teacher-specific residuals to account for cross-evidence agreement and conflict. It uses verification outcomes and evidence availability to choose between reward-anchored refinement and reverse-KL correction, reserving the latter for eligible verified failures. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Overview 

We propose MeSD, a multi-evidence self-distillation framework designed for VideoLLMs. The core challenge is to integrate heterogeneous privileged evidence into token-level supervision and determine how this supervision should guide trajectories with different verification outcomes. As illustrated in Figure 2, MeSD constructs Evidence-Conditioned Teachers from answer, temporal, and spatial annotations, with shared EMA parameters. These teachers evaluate reasoning trajectories sampled by the student from video-question pairs. Teacher Distribution Fusion compares their predictions at the same student-generated prefixes and aggregates evidence-specific preferences into a fused teacher distribution. Verification-Guided Optimization uses a task-specific verifier to classify each trajectory as Success, Failure, or Indeterminate. For Success and Indeterminate trajectories, token-level advantage refinement adjusts advantage magnitudes while preserving their reward-derived signs. For verified failures with the required evidence, failure-conditioned distillation replaces the GRPO policy loss with a reverse-KL objective targeting the fused teacher distribution.

Integrating these evidence sources within a single teacher context obscures their relationship and makes their individual contributions hard to distinguish. # 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup  

> Implementation Details.

MeSD is initialized from Open-o3-Video-SFT-7B and trained on STGR-RL (Meng et al., 2025) for one epoch. For a fair comparison, we use the same training data and annotations, with existing answer and grounding labels serving as teacher privileged information. We generate four on-policy responses per prompt using vLLM at temperature 1.0, with a maximum response length of 768 tokens. Videos are sampled at 2 FPS with at most 16 frames. The learning rate is 10 −6, and token weights are clipped to 

[0 .8, 1.2] . The teacher reweighting coefficient λs decays linearly from 0.5 to zero over the first 1000 steps. Eligible failures use reverse-KL distillation in place of the GRPO policy loss, with γs increasing linearly from zero to 0.8 over the first 100 steps. After step 1000, teacher supervision is disabled and training continues with standard GRPO. Further experimental details are provided in Appendix D.

Effects of Evidence Separation and Complementarity. Table 4 compares MeSD with two ablated variants: a single teacher conditioned on all available evidence (w/o ES) and an answer-only teacher (w/o T&S). Both variants underperform compared to MeSD across all metrics. Notably, the TVGBench mIoU drops from 29.8% to 22.4% and 22.3%, respectively. These results support incorporating temporal and spatial evidence and modeling their preferences separately. 

Effects of Gated Preference Aggregation. With the evidence-conditioned teachers fixed, we replace the gated preference aggregation with a simple arithmetic averaging of the evidence-specific preferences (w/o GPA). This modification degrades performance across all benchmarks (Table 4). Specifically, V-STAR mLGM drops from 50.2% to 41.4%, and TVGBench mIoU decreases from 29.8% to 22.9%. These results demonstrate the advantages of selectively retaining teacher-specific support while suppressing conflicting contributions. 

Effects of Failure-Conditioned Distillation. Applying token-level advantage refinement to all trajectories without FCD reduces the V-STAR mLGM and TVGBench mIoU by 9.2% and 6.8%, respectively (Table 4). The training curves exhibit a similar pattern: the model without FCD reaches a lower reward plateau, whereas the MeSD reward continues to increase before stabilizing (Figure 3(a)). These results indicate that the corrective guidance provided by advantage refinement for failed trajectories is inherently limited. Failure-conditioned distillation employs the fused teacher distribution for reverse-KL correction, thereby providing supervision over candidate tokens that extends beyond sampled-token reweighting.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced MeSD, a multi-evidence self-distillation framework for VideoLLMs that combines evidence-aware supervision with verification-guided optimization. Using an answer-conditioned teacher as a semantic anchor, MeSD integrates temporal and spatial evidence through teacher-common preferences and gated teacher-specific residuals. It preserves reward-derived advantage signs during credit refinement and reserves reverse-KL correction for verified failures with the required evidence. Experiments demonstrate overall gains over evaluated baselines across video understanding, spatiotemporal reasoning, and temporal grounding, including improvements across Qwen3-VL model sizes. Ablations support evidence separation, gated fusion, and selective correction for effective video post-training using existing annotations.

# A Extended Related Work 

A.1 Post-Training for VideoLLMs 

RLVR supports VideoLLM post-training through outcome-level feedback on model-generated trajectories. Video-R1 (Feng et al., 2025) encourages temporal reasoning, VideoRFT (Wang et al., 2025a) rewards consistency between reasoning and visual evidence, and DeepVideo-R1 (Park et al., 2025) improves opti-mization through a regressive objective and difficulty-aware augmentation. For localization, Time-R1 (Wang et al., 2025b) targets temporal grounding, while VideoChat-R1 (Li et al., 2025) strengthens spatiotemporal perception through multi-task reinforcement fine-tuning. Video-OPD (Li et al., 2026) provides dense teacher guidance for efficient temporal grounding, and VisionCoach (Lee et al., 2026) combines visual prompting, grounding rewards, and self-distillation to improve evidence localization. Open-o3 Video (Meng et al., 2025) unifies answer and spatiotemporal supervision through reasoning traces containing timestamps, objects, and bounding boxes, with rewards jointly evaluating answer correctness and grounding quality. MeSD follows this paradigm using the same training data and annotations. It organizes answer and grounding information into evidence-conditioned self-teacher contexts for token-level guidance along student-generated trajectories. 

A.2 On-Policy and Privileged-Context Self-Distillation 

On-policy distillation addresses the mismatch between distillation sequences and student-generated sequences at inference. Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD) (Agarwal et al., 2024) provides teacher-distribution supervision at student-generated prefixes. Video-OPD (Li et al., 2026) applies this principle using a stronger external teacher. On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026) uses the same model under different contexts, conditioning the teacher on privileged information, such as reference solutions, to supervise student rollouts. Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) (Hübotter et al., 2026) converts textual environment feedback into dense supervision through a feedback-conditioned self-teacher. Both derive teacher guidance from contextual information without a separately trained, stronger model. Multimodal self-distillation extends this principle to visual evidence. Vision-OPD (Yuan et al., 2026) transfers crop-conditioned predictions to a student processing full images. Video-OPSD (Wang et al., 2026) conditions its self-teacher on annotated evidence frames and weights distillation by token reliance on visual evidence. S³T 

(Venkatraman et al., 2026) uses denser temporal sampling to supervise a sparse-view student without labels or rewards. VISD (Lin et al., 2026) incorporates structured feedback from a video-aware judge to diagnose answer consistency and spatiotemporal grounding. These methods construct visual self-teachers through distinct contextual evidence. MeSD organizes existing answer and grounding annotations into separate contexts to explicitly compare and fuse evidence-conditioned teacher preferences. 

A.3 Evidence Aggregation and Adaptive Supervision 

Multi-teacher on-policy distillation combines complementary capabilities on student-generated trajectories.  

> MOPD

(Ma et al., 2026) merges independently trained specialists into one student, while MT-SDPO (He et al., 2026) conditions an EMA self-teacher on verified teacher feedback. AVSD (Nguyen et al., 2026) aggregates privileged views for self-distillation. A single video-evidence context leaves source contributions and cross-evidence agreement, complementarity, and conflict implicit. MeSD separates evidence-conditioned views and fuses temporal and spatial preference shifts relative to an answer-conditioned semantic anchor. For adaptive supervision, VG-OPD (Xu and Zhang, 2026) uses verification to select eligible experts and localize token-level supervision. RLSD (Yang et al., 2026) and VISD (Lin et al., 2026) refine token credit while preserving reward-derived update directions; H²SD (Cai et al., 2026) explores outcome-dependent asymmetric self-distillation. Failure feedback alone does not identify preferred alternatives. Based on verification and evidence availability, MeSD uses its fused teacher distribution for reward-anchored credit refinement on successful and indeterminate trajectories, and reverse-KL correction only on eligible, verified failures.

All contexts retain the original task-specific system prompt, visual input, and question, while appending different privileged evidence to the user message. The Answer context provides the verified semantic answer. These boxes are expressed in the pixel coordinates of the processed input image. The accompanying prompt templates illustrate the three contexts for joint spatio-temporal question answering. All three contexts are evaluated by the same EMA self-teacher on the same student-generated prefix, without generating additional teacher rollouts. The privileged evidence is used only during training and is not provided to the student at inference time.

C.2 Trajectory Verification 

We distinguish three decisions: outcome verification assigns a trajectory label, data validation determines whether the training example is usable, and correction selection determines whether to apply failure-conditioned distillation.  

> Outcome verification.

Unlike sequence-level rewards, which provide graded feedback for policy optimization, verification assigns Success, Failure, or Indeterminate. For supported tasks, a trajectory is labeled Success if all required components pass, Failure if any component explicitly fails, and Indeterminate otherwise. The verifier requires exactly one nonempty <answer> block. Multiple-choice outputs must contain a parseable option from A–H. Temporal outputs must contain exactly one interval in the expected timestamp format, with finite endpoints and an end time strictly greater than the start time. For temporal multiple-choice QA, the option and interval are extracted separately from the Correct Option: field and the answer block. An unparseable component satisfies neither the success nor the failure criterion; another explicitly incorrect component can still establish Failure. Missing or malformed intervals are never treated as zero-overlap predictions. Tasks without explicit verification rules retain their task-specific rewards for policy optimization.  

> Data validity.

Malformed model outputs are distinct from invalid training data. The former may remain valid trajectories with uncertain outcomes, whereas unparseable ground-truth options, invalid ground-truth intervals, and visual loading failures are flagged as invalid. Visual loading failures additionally receive a skip flag. Any Indeterminate label attached to an invalid row is a placeholder, not an uncertain prediction on valid data. Required reward metadata is also validated. Spatial localization requires annotation and processed image sizes. Temporal–spatial free-form QA requires frame sizes, nonempty keyframe annotations, an object annotation mapping, and training progress for reward scheduling. Under strict validation, missing or malformed required metadata raises an error rather than silently yielding a zero reward.  

> Correction selection.

A Failure label alone does not guarantee RKL eligibility. In particular, temporal multiple-choice QA requires both an incorrect option and a valid interval with zero tIoU under the strict setting. Responses with only one incorrect component are therefore labeled Failure but excluded from correction. Let vi indicate data validity, ei denote outcome-based eligibility from Table 7, and hi indicate whether the teacher evidence required to construct a correction target is available. During teacher supervision, the correction indicator is 

gi = vieihi. (17) Evidence availability is checked independently of the verification outcome. For valid trajectories, gi = 1 selects failure-conditioned distillation in place of the policy loss, whereas gi = 0 

retains token-level advantage refinement. This fallback includes Success and Indeterminate trajectories, failures excluded by the strict eligibility rule, and otherwise eligible failures lacking the required teacher evidence. If no usable teacher signal is available for refinement, the original reward-derived advantage is retained.

# D Evaluation Details 

Our evaluation examines whether multi-evidence self-distillation improves answer correctness and spatio-temporal localization, and whether these gains extend to broader video understanding tasks. We describe the benchmarks, comparison methods, and evaluation metrics below. 

> 20 MeSD: Multi-Evidence Self-Distillation for VideoLLM

D.1 Benchmarks  

> Joint spatiotemporal reasoning.

V-STaR (Cheng et al., 2026) jointly evaluates question answering and the localization of supporting temporal and spatial evidence. It contains two reasoning chains: what–when–where and what–where–when. We report results for both chains to assess MeSD under different evidence prediction orders and distinguish gains in answer accuracy from those in temporal and spatial grounding.  

> Temporal grounding.

We use TVGBench (Wang et al., 2025b) and Charades-STA (Gao et al., 2017) to evaluate temporal localization beyond joint reasoning. TVGBench contains temporal grounding examples from multiple source datasets, while Charades-STA requires identifying the video interval described by a natural-language query. These benchmarks complement V-STaR by directly assessing how well predicted intervals align with annotated events.  

> General and long-video understanding.

We use WorldSense (Hong et al., 2026), VideoMMMU (Hu et al., 2025), and LongVideo-Reason-eval (LRR) (Chen et al., 2025) to assess whether evidence-based training also improves video question answering. WorldSense covers recognition, understanding, and reasoning. VideoMMMU evaluates knowledge acquisition from professional videos through perception, comprehension, and adaptation, while LRR tests reasoning over long videos. We also evaluate on Video-MME-v2 (Fu et al., 2026), which uses groups of related questions to assess capability consistency and reasoning coherence. Together, these benchmarks assess whether MeSD’s benefits extend beyond explicit grounding. 

D.2 Baselines  

> General and video-specialized models.

We include Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Bai et al., 2025b), InternVL-2.5-8B (Chen et al., 2024), and Oryx-1.5-7B (Liu et al., 2025), alongside video-specialized models such as VideoChat2 (Li et al., 2024), Video-LLaMA3 (Zhang et al., 2025a), LLaVA-Video (Zhang et al., 2025b), and TRACE (Guo et al., 2025d). We also report results for GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) and Gemini-2-Flash (Google DeepMind, 2025) as proprietary references where available. For Charades-STA, we include temporal grounding methods such as TimeChat (Ren et al., 2024), HawkEye (Wang et al., 2024), VTG-LLM (Guo et al., 2025c), and VTimeLLM (Huang et al., 2024). These comparisons position MeSD relative to a broad range of video understanding and localization methods.  

> Video post-training and self-distillation.

Our main post-training baselines include VideoRFT (Wang et al., 2025a), Video-R1 (Feng et al., 2025), Open-o3-Video (Meng et al., 2025), VisionCoach (Lee et al., 2026), and VISD (Lin et al., 2026). Our training setup builds on Open-o3-Video’s grounded video reasoning framework. VISD serves as a closely related self-distillation baseline, using teacher-derived signals to refine token-level credit while preserving reward-derived update directions. Comparing MeSD with VISD assesses the added value of separating evidence-conditioned teacher preferences and introducing distribution-level correction for eligible, verified failures.  

> Optimization and component comparisons.

We also include GRPO and GSPO as reward-based optimization baselines. Component ablations individually assess evidence separation, temporal and spatial supervision, gated preference aggregation, and failure-conditioned distillation. These comparisons complement the model-level results by identifying the contributions of individual MeSD components.

# H Limitations and Future Work 

While MeSD effectively integrates answer, temporal, and spatial annotations, the current study explores only a subset of the evidence that could support video reasoning. Its multi-evidence formulation offers a natural basis for incorporating richer supervision, but the potential benefits of additional annotation types remain unexplored. The present evaluation therefore leaves open how greater annotation diversity and finer granularity could further improve self-distillation. Future work could introduce complementary annotations, such as object trajectories and event relations, to construct additional evidence-conditioned teacher views. Extending the fusion mechanism to account for differences in evidence granularity and reliability could help integrate these sources effectively. Systematically examining the contributions of different annotation types and their combinations would further clarify how richer supervision supports video reasoning. Such extensions may provide more detailed guidance for complex temporal and spatial reasoning and broaden the applicability of MeSD.
